# Mud Season

There are five seasons here, not four, and the fifth one has no poems written about it. It comes after the snow and before anything green. The frost goes out of the ground from the top down, so for three or four weeks the dirt roads sit on a sheet of ice that hasn't thawed yet, and the water has nowhere to go. The road turns to pudding. Then it turns to something worse than pudding.

I have seen a school bus sunk to its axles on the Hollow Road. I have seen a man lose a boot, just step out of it, and leave it standing in the ruts like a fence post. (He went back for it in May. It was still there.) The town posts the roads with orange signs, no trucks over six tons, and the log trucks go around by the state highway, grumbling, and everybody who lives past the pavement parks at the bottom and walks up.

That walk is the whole of mud season, as far as I'm concerned. You carry your groceries a quarter mile uphill in rubber boots. You meet your neighbors doing the same thing. Nobody is in a hurry, because you can't be; the mud won't allow it. So you stop and talk. You find out who had the flu, whose sap buckets are running, who saw the first redwing blackbird down by the beaver pond. I've learned more about this town standing in the mud than I ever learned at Town Meeting.

Summer people don't see any of this. They come in June, when the road has been graded and the lupines are out, and they think the place has always looked like a calendar. I don't blame them. If I were selling the town I'd sell it in June, too. But I'd feel a little dishonest about it.

What mud season teaches, if it teaches anything, is that you don't control much. You can pave the road, I suppose. Some towns have. Then you get frost heaves instead, and potholes you could lose a dog in, and a bill every spring from the paving contractor. Or you can leave it gravel and wait. The ground thaws when it thaws. The water drains when it drains. One morning, usually in the third week of April, you drive to the bottom of the hill out of habit and realize you could have kept going; the road has firmed up overnight, and the season is over, and nobody announced it.

My wife used to say that mud season was the only honest time of year. Everything else was dressed up for somebody: the leaves in October for the tourists, the snow at Christmas for the cards. Mud was just mud. It didn't care whether you liked it. I think she was right, although she said it mostly to annoy her sister, who lived in Connecticut and came up only for foliage.

I keep a pair of boots by the door that I use for nothing else. They are the ugliest boots I own, patched twice at the heel, and I would not trade them for anything in the catalog. When I pull them on the first time each spring I feel something close to relief. Winter is finally letting go. It's letting go badly, and messily, and all over the kitchen floor; but it is letting go.

So I don't mind the mud, most years. I mind it in the fourth week, when I'm tired of it, and I mind it when I get the truck stuck, which I do about once a spring out of stubbornness. The rest of the time I think of it as the price of the other seasons. You can't have the one without the other. Nobody gets June for free.
