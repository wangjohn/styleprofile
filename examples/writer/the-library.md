# The Library

Our library is one room and a closet. It was built in 1903 with money left by a woman named Harriet Coburn, who had no children and, according to the plaque by the door, "a lively regard for the improvement of the young." I have always liked that phrase. It suggests she knew the young needed improving and had no great hopes of it.

The building is brick, which is unusual around here, with a slate roof and a round window over the door that nobody has ever been able to clean from the inside. There is a woodstove in the corner that hasn't been lit since the town put in baseboard heat, and a long oak table with initials carved into its underside going back a hundred years. I found my own there once, lying on my back with a flashlight while the librarian pretended not to notice. They were exactly where I'd left them in 1952.

The librarian now is a young woman from Burlington who came up for one year and stayed for eleven. She has done more for the place than anyone since Harriet Coburn. She got the roof fixed. She started a reading hour for the little ones on Saturday mornings, and a knitting group on Tuesdays that is, as far as I can tell, mostly a gossip group with yarn. She put in two computers, which I don't use, and a table of seed packets in the spring that you can take for free if you bring back seeds in the fall. I bring back beans. She says I'm the only one who does.

I go twice a week. I read the newspapers, which I won't pay for, and I take out one book and bring back one book, and I talk to whoever's there. Sometimes that's nobody. Sometimes it's the Pike boys, who are twelve and thirteen and come in after school to use the computers and argue in whispers. Sometimes it's old Dorothy Rich, who's ninety-four and reads a mystery a day and tells me the endings before I can stop her. I've quit reading mysteries. There's no point.

What a small library gives you is not books, exactly. You can get books anywhere now; they come in the mail, or on a screen. What it gives you is a room where nobody is trying to sell you anything. I didn't understand how rare that was until I'd lived long enough to see most of the other rooms go. The grange hall closed. The store went to a chain and then went to nothing. The churches are down to a handful on Sunday. The library is still open Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday, and you can walk in and sit at that oak table as long as you like, and the only thing asked of you is that you keep your voice down.

Town Meeting argues about the library budget every March. Somebody always stands up and says it's a luxury, and the library ought to be run by volunteers, and anyway everything's on the internet. Somebody else always stands up and says the internet doesn't have a stove or a table or a woman from Burlington who knows your name. Then we vote, and the library gets its money, a little less than it asked for and a little more than the man who called it a luxury wanted. It's been going that way for as long as I've been voting. I expect it will go that way after I'm gone.

When I die I've told my son to give my books to the library. Most of them it won't want. That's fine; it can sell them at the August book sale for a quarter apiece, and some summer person will take home my copy of Thoreau with the pencil marks in it and wonder who underlined all the parts about beans.
