# The Woodstove

The stove in my kitchen is older than I am. It's a cast-iron parlor stove with nickel trim that went dull before Eisenhower, and a door that closes only if you lift it a quarter inch and lean on it. My grandfather bought it secondhand. I have never once thought about replacing it, although my son brings me brochures every Christmas for stoves with glass fronts and catalytic converters and I thank him and put them in the kindling box.

Heating with wood is not efficient. I know that. You cut the trees, or you buy them cut, and then you split them and stack them and let them dry for a year, and then you carry them in, an armload at a time, and feed them to the stove, and carry the ashes back out. You're handling the same piece of wood five or six times before it gives you anything. An oil furnace would do the job with a thermostat and a phone call. But an oil furnace doesn't give you anything to do on a November afternoon.

I split my own wood until I was seventy-four. Now a boy from down the road does it for me, and I pay him more than it's worth because he stacks it the way I like: bark up, ends out, a row of crosshatched pieces at each end so the pile doesn't lean. He thinks I'm particular. I am. A good woodpile is the only thing I've ever built that I could look at from the window and feel entirely satisfied with. It's square. It's full. It will be gone by March.

There's a rhythm to a woodstove day that I have come to depend on. You bank it at night with a couple of big pieces of maple and close the damper almost all the way; in the morning there are coals under the ash, red as a cut, and you open the draft and lay on some small split stuff and in ten minutes the kettle is ticking. By noon you're down to a steady fire. By four o'clock, when the light goes, you build it up again. The house warms from the kitchen outward, never evenly. The back bedroom is always cold. I sleep there anyway. I like the difference.

My wife hated the stove for the first ten years we were married. She said it made the whole house smell like a campfire, which it does, and that it put a film of ash on everything, which it also does. Then one winter the power went out for nine days after an ice storm. Every house on the road with a furnace went cold, and every one of those families ended up in our kitchen at one point or another, eating soup she made on the stovetop. After that she never said a word against it. She didn't say anything for it, either. She just started keeping a pot of something on the back corner.

People ask me if I worry about chimney fires. Of course I do. I have the flue cleaned every fall, and I burn dry wood, and I listen. A chimney fire sounds like a freight train in the wall; you don't mistake it once you've heard it. I heard it once, in 1981, and I've been careful ever since.

I suppose the real reason I keep the stove is that it asks something of me. Most things in a house don't, anymore. The refrigerator hums along by itself. The furnace, if I had one, would too. The stove wants attention three or four times a day, and it rewards attention and punishes neglect, and it doesn't care how old I am. That seems fair to me. I'd like to think I'll keep feeding it until I can't, and then somebody else will lift the door a quarter inch and lean on it.
