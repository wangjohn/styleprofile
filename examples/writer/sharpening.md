# Sharpening

There is a stone on my workbench that I have been using for forty years, and it has a hollow worn in the middle of it like a saddle. That hollow is the only record I have of every axe and chisel and pocketknife I've owned. I've never counted them. The stone knows.

Most people don't sharpen anything anymore. When a kitchen knife gets dull they buy a new one, or they run it through one of those gadgets with two little wheels that chews the edge into something like a saw. I don't say this to be superior. I understand it. Sharpening takes time, and it takes a kind of patience that doesn't come naturally to anybody, and for most of history nobody did it for fun. They did it because the knife was the only knife they had.

My father taught me on a hatchet. I was eight or nine. He showed me how to hold the angle, which was the whole trick, he said: pick an angle and keep it, stroke after stroke, and don't rock. I rocked. Everybody rocks at first. You end up with an edge that's round instead of flat, and round edges don't cut; they just push. He let me ruin that hatchet for most of an afternoon and then he took it back and fixed it in about four minutes without saying a word. I was furious. I've since done the same thing to my son, and my son has done it to his daughter, so I suppose it's a family method.

The feeling you're after is hard to describe. When you've brought the edge up far enough on one side, a tiny lip of metal curls over onto the other side. It's called a burr. You can't see it, most of the time, but you can feel it if you draw your thumb across the edge (across, never along). It catches, like the edge of a fingernail. That's how you know you've done enough. Then you turn the blade over and take the burr off, and then you do it again on a finer stone, and again, and every time the burr gets smaller, until there's nothing left to feel and the edge simply bites.

I sharpen on Sunday evenings, mostly, when there's nothing on the radio. It's quiet work. The only sound is the stone, a kind of whisper, steady as breathing. I don't think about much. I think about the angle. If I start thinking about something else (money, or my knees, or the news) the angle wanders and I have to start over. So I don't. For half an hour I think about nothing but a strip of steel a few thousandths of an inch wide, and at the end of it I feel better than I did before. I suspect that's the real reason I keep doing it.

A sharp tool is safer than a dull one. Everybody who's used both knows this, and nobody believes it until they've been cut. A dull axe glances off a knot and comes around at your shin. A dull knife needs pushing, and when it slips you're pushing it into your own hand. A sharp one goes where you send it. My grandfather used to say a dull axe was the most dangerous thing on a farm, after a bull and a hired man with a grudge.

I've given away most of my tools now. My grandson has the good chisels. The axes went to the boy who splits my wood. I kept the stone, though, and a couple of knives, and the hatchet my father taught me on, which is short a quarter inch from all the times it's been ground back. It isn't much of a hatchet anymore. It's the sharpest thing in the house.
