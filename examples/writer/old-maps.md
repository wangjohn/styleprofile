# Old Maps

The best map of this town was drawn in 1869 by a man named Beers, who never lived here and probably spent no more than a week riding around with a notebook. It hangs in the town office, behind glass that has gone a little yellow, and every house on it has a black square and a name. J. Wheelock. Wid. Parmenter. S.H. Bixby, Blacksmith. There are forty-one schoolhouses in the county on that map. There are three now.

I used to stand in front of it while I waited for the clerk to find me a deed. It never got old. You'd see a square marked on a road that doesn't exist anymore, or on a road that's now just a line of stone walls going up through the woods, and you'd think: somebody lived there. They milked cows and buried children and argued about the minister, right there, where there's nothing now but beech saplings and a cellar hole with a lilac growing out of it. Lilacs are the last thing to go. You can find an old farm by them, in May, when nothing else is blooming in the woods.

Old maps are wrong in interesting ways. Beers put the brook on the wrong side of the Tabor place. He spelled Wheelock two different ways on the same sheet. His compass was off by a couple of degrees, which he didn't correct for, so everything on the east side of the town is rotated a little, as if the whole valley had turned in its sleep. I've never minded. A modern map is right about everything and tells you almost nothing. It shows where the roads go. It doesn't show who lived at the end of them.

My father had a map he'd drawn himself, in pencil, on the back of a feed-store calendar. It showed our land and the land on either side, and it marked things no surveyor would bother with: the spring that never froze, the big white oak the lightning hit, a spot by the brook labeled only "trout?" (with the question mark). He kept it in a drawer in the kitchen table. When he died I found it there, folded in quarters, soft as cloth. I have it in the same drawer now. I've added a few things in ink. I didn't feel right using pencil.

There's a kind of person who loves maps and a kind who doesn't, and I don't think you can turn one into the other. My wife could get lost in a parking lot. She navigated by landmarks and by asking people, and she usually got where she was going faster than I did, because while I was studying the map she was already talking to somebody at a gas station who knew a shortcut. I'd tell her the shortcut wasn't on the map. She'd tell me that was the point of a shortcut.

What I like about an old map, finally, is that it's an argument. Somebody decided what mattered. Beers thought the blacksmith mattered, and the schoolhouses, and the names of the men who owned the land (rarely the women, unless they were widows). He didn't think the Abenaki trail along the river mattered, so it isn't there, though it was still in use when he drew the thing. You learn to read the gaps, after a while, the same way you learn to read the stones in a wall.

I've been asked, now and then, to give a talk at the historical society about the Beers map. I always say no. I'm not a historian; I just looked at it a lot. But if I did give the talk, I think I'd bring my father's calendar map, too, and lay them side by side, and let people decide which one told them more.
