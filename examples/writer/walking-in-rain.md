# Walking in the Rain

I walk every morning, and I walk in the rain when it rains. This is not a virtue. I just found out, somewhere around the age of sixty, that the rain didn't hurt me, and that the walk was better with it than without.

The first thing rain does is empty the road. Nobody else is out. The dog walkers stay in, and the fellow who jogs past my house at seven in a shirt that says something about a marathon he ran in 1996 stays in, and the log trucks slow down. You have the whole valley to yourself. It's a strange kind of wealth. I don't know of another way to get it.

The second thing rain does is change the sound of everything. On a dry day the woods are full of small noises you don't notice: a squirrel, a jay, the wind in the tops of the pines. In the rain all of that goes under a steady hiss, and what comes through it is louder and closer. Water running in the ditch. Drips off the hemlocks, which are bigger and slower than the rain itself, so you hear them separately, one at a time, like somebody tapping on a table. The brook, if it's been raining long, sounds like traffic.

I wear an old canvas coat that my wife waxed for me the year before she died. It doesn't keep out much anymore. I don't mind. After about ten minutes you stop being someone who is getting wet and become someone who is wet, and there's a freedom in that. You can't get any wetter. You might as well look around.

What you see, if you look, is color. Rain brings it up. The bark on the maples goes nearly black, and the lichen on the stone walls turns a green you'd swear was painted. Moss swells up like a sponge. The dirt road goes from gray to the brown of strong coffee. Even the dead leaves in November look better wet; they lie flat and shine, the red ones especially, like something spilled.

My neighbor, who is a sensible woman, thinks I'm going to catch pneumonia. She tells me so from her porch, under the eave, holding her coffee. I tell her I've been walking in the rain for fifteen years and I haven't caught anything yet. She says that's what everybody says right up until they do. She may be right. I have noticed, though, that she watches for me, and on the mornings I'm late she comes out to the end of her drive to look down the road. I've never mentioned it. I don't think she knows I've seen her.

There are rains I don't walk in. Thunder keeps me home; I'm stubborn, not stupid. So does ice, since a man my age who falls on ice doesn't always get up again. And there's a particular cold rain in late March, just above freezing, driven sideways by a north wind, that I'll admit defeats me. On those days I stand at the kitchen window with my coffee and feel as if I've missed something.

But an ordinary rain, a summer rain or a soft gray autumn one, is the best walking weather there is. I'd say so to anyone who asked. Nobody asks. They look at me coming back up the road, dripping, with my hat brim running like a gutter, and they decide I'm a little odd, and they're not wrong. I go inside and hang the coat by the stove and it steams for an hour. The whole kitchen smells of wet canvas and wax. I like that, too.
