# The Hardware Store

The hardware store in the village has been in the same family since 1938, and it still sells nails by the pound. You scoop them out of a wooden bin with a tin scoop and Walt weighs them on a scale that was old when his father bought it. He writes the price on the bag in grease pencil. I have never once seen him use the register for nails.

It's a narrow building, long and dim, with a pressed-tin ceiling and floorboards that dip in front of the counter where eighty years of boots have worn them down. Everything is in there somewhere. Stovepipe, canning lids, fly strips, chain by the foot, a drawer of hinges nobody has asked for since the war. If Walt doesn't have it he'll tell you who does, and if nobody has it he'll tell you how to make do without it, which is the more useful answer anyway.

I go in for one thing and come out an hour later with the one thing. The hour is for talking. Walt knows who's building, who's selling, whose well went dry, and he'll tell you, but only if you stand there long enough. He doesn't hurry. Nobody in the store hurries. There's a bench by the stove where the retired men sit on winter mornings and settle the affairs of the state, and I've sat on it myself, more than I'd care to say.

But the store is more than a place to buy things — it's a vital part of the community's fabric. In an era of big-box retailers and online shopping, small independent stores like this one play a crucial role in keeping rural towns connected. They offer personalized service, local knowledge, and a sense of belonging that simply can't be replicated elsewhere.

The big store in the city is cheaper. I know that; everybody knows it. It's forty minutes each way and they have everything, in quantities you'd need a truck for, under lights that make my eyes ache. Nobody there knows what a stake and stones is. I went once for a length of pipe and a boy in a vest walked me to the wrong aisle and then disappeared, and I stood there for a while among the patio furniture feeling about a hundred years old.

Walt is seventy-one. His daughter works the counter on Saturdays, and she's good, and she's told him she'll take it on. I hope she does. I hope she keeps the nail bins and the tin scoop and the scale, and the bench by the stove, even though the bench doesn't sell anything and never has. Some things earn their keep in other ways.

Ultimately, the future of the village hardware store depends on all of us. By choosing to shop locally, we can help ensure that these beloved institutions continue to thrive for generations to come.

I bought a pound of eightpenny nails there last week. I didn't need them. I have a coffee can full of nails in the shed, most of them bent. But I wanted to hear the scoop go into the bin, and watch Walt squint at the scale, and see him write the price on the bag. Sixty cents. He rounded down.
