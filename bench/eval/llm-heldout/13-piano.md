# Learning Piano

I started piano lessons as an adult because I'd regretted not learning as a child, and I was finally old enough to stop caring whether the regret made sense. A regret is a regret. I found a teacher who taught adults, scheduled a weekly lesson, and committed to showing up.

The first thing the teacher explained was that adult learning is different from child learning. Children can make mistakes for months and still eventually internalize patterns through repetition. Adults notice the mistakes immediately and get frustrated. The compensation is that adults can understand the structure of what they're learning, can ask why the patterns work the way they do, can practice more efficiently.

The early weeks were about learning to read music and getting my hands to sit correctly on the keyboard. The posture was surprising—not as upright as I expected, with the wrists held at a specific angle. The reading felt remedial. It was. I was essentially learning an alphabet at age forty. But the teacher was patient. She would wait while I identified notes, didn't correct me before I could try.

I was determined not to give up during the inevitable frustration period. This is the stage where you realize how much coordination is required, where your hands don't quite do what you want, where the gap between your understanding of music and your ability to produce it is humiliatingly large. I prepared myself mentally for this period. When it came, I was able to move through it. The frustration was real, but I'd expected it.

After maybe three months, something shifted. The knowledge of where the notes were became less a conscious thing and more automatic. I could read simple pieces without having to identify every note individually. My right hand could play a melody while my left hand did something else, which felt miraculous. I would sit down to practice and an hour would pass without my noticing.

The pieces I could play were simple—folk songs, simple classical pieces, arrangements that were almost laughably basic. But they were real music. I could sit at the piano and create something that sounded intentional, that had melody and some harmonic structure. The pleasure of this was unexpected and outsized relative to how little I was actually accomplishing.

One challenge was practice consistency. The improvement was real, but only if I practiced regularly. Missing a week meant losing more progress than a week of practice created. The teacher would gently point this out when my technique had degraded. The implication was clear: this will only work if you prioritize it.

I had to think about whether I was willing to make that commitment. I had limited free time. Taking an hour or two a week for practice meant not doing something else. I decided that yes, I was willing. Learning piano became something I did, the way other people run or paint or write. It wasn't a major life focus, but it was a regular discipline.

A year in, I can play simple pieces competently. I can read music reliably. I understand basic music theory. I'm not good—not by any standard that would matter to anyone else. But I'm learning, and the process has been rewarding.

What surprised me most was that it's possible to start as an adult. I had assumed that learning music was something you did as a child or not at all. But adults can learn. It's slower. It requires more intentionality. It requires accepting that you'll be bad at something for a while before you improve. But it's possible.

I don't know if I'll be playing piano a decade from now. But right now, once a week, I sit down with my teacher and work on understanding how to make the keys produce the sounds I want. The progress is slow, but it's real. And that's enough.
