# First Sourdough

The recipe looked simple enough: flour, water, salt, and a starter culture that was supposedly alive and would do the work. I'd been given a jar of starter by a friend who swore it was foolproof. "You just feed it and use it," she said. "It's basically unkillable."

I fed it as instructed, and it bubbled. This seemed promising.

The instructions said to mix the ingredients and let them sit for four hours. I did this. The dough rose slightly, developed some strength in the gluten. Then I shaped it and put it in the fridge overnight, and the next morning I baked it.

The bread came out of the oven looking like bread. It had a crust. It had some height. I was optimistic. Then I cut into it and found that the inside was dense and gummy, with barely any open crumb structure. It tasted okay—definitely like sourdough, definitely like bread—but it was heavy. It was more like a brick than the airy loaf I'd seen people make.

I tried again. Same result.

The problem, it turned out, was that I didn't understand the timing. The rise that happened in the four hours wasn't enough. The dough needed more time, or warmer conditions, or both. I was letting the yeast work, but not enough. I was creating structure but then collapsing it with my handling.

The second attempt was better because I let the dough rise longer. The third was better still because I paid attention to when the dough was actually doubled in size instead of just waiting the time the recipe said. By the fourth loaf, I was starting to understand something about how dough actually moves and changes as it rises.

By the sixth loaf, I made something that actually looked and tasted like sourdough—light, with actual holes in the crumb structure, a developed flavor from the long fermentation.

What surprised me was how much intuition is required in baking. The recipe is just a framework. The actual timing depends on temperature, humidity, how hydrated the dough is, how active your starter is. You have to learn to read the dough itself, to understand when it's actually ready rather than trusting that the timer on your phone knows.

This is frustrating for someone like me who wants instructions and predictability. But it's also weirdly satisfying. Once you start understanding the patterns, baking becomes less like following a recipe and more like having a conversation with the dough. You observe how it's behaving and respond to that.

The nice thing is that even bad sourdough is still bread. Even my dense, gummy first loaves were edible. They weren't great, but they worked fine for toast or as a base for something else. The learning curve wasn't punishing.

Now, six months later, I make bread most weeks. It's become a rhythm. Mix on one day, shape the next morning, bake the next afternoon. The bread is usually good. Sometimes it's very good. Sometimes I try something new and it doesn't work as well as I hoped. But the process itself has become something I look forward to—the time spent handling dough, understanding how it changes, creating something to eat.
