# A Year of Working Remote

I spent a year working entirely from home, and it changed my understanding of what work is. Not in a simple way—not as a straightforward good or bad—but as something more complex about the structure of time and attention and presence.

The obvious benefits were real. I saved two hours a day that would have gone to commuting. This time was mine. I could eat lunch in my kitchen, work outside on good weather days, take a walk at three in the afternoon if I felt restless. There was no question of whether I could make a dentist appointment at two o'clock—of course I could, and I could go back to work afterwards without any transition.

The flexibility changed the texture of the day. You could start work at seven in the morning and stop at two in the afternoon if that's what fit your rhythm. Or you could work irregularly, in focused bursts separated by breaks that actually broke your focus instead of just moving you to a different location. The structure became something you created instead of something imposed.

The financial benefit was not nothing. The commute cost gas and parking and vehicle wear. Lunch out every day added up. Even small things—a coffee before work, a snack in the afternoon because the vending machine was right there—these disappeared. A year of remote work was a thousand or two dollars richer just from reduced spending.

But it was also lonely in a particular way.

The lack of casual human contact was stranger than I expected. Not the meetings—those happened via video call and were almost worse than in-person because you couldn't see most of the room, couldn't overhear conversations, couldn't bump into someone in the hallway and have a quick chat. But the missing moments between tasks, the time spent around other people without directly interacting with them, the sense of being in a shared space—all of that was gone.

The boundary between work and home blurred. Your bedroom was your office was your kitchen. You could see your unmade bed from your desk. The coffee maker was ten feet away. It became hard to have a sense of "being at work" or "being home" because they were the same place. This could be comfortable, but it could also be exhausting. There was no moment of transition, no journey that marked the shift from work mode to home mode.

I also learned something about collaboration. Much of our work is done in writing, and writing can happen remotely. But there's a kind of thinking that happens in person that doesn't quite translate. The whiteboard brainstorm where people are building ideas together in real time. The conversation where someone says something that sparks an idea in someone else, and that idea gets built on by a third person. These are possible on video calls, but they're slower and less organic. Some part of the collaborative energy is lost.

What I discovered over the year was that remote work and office work are not simply different versions of the same thing. They're different ways of working. Remote work optimizes for focus and flexibility. Office work optimizes for serendipitous connection and rapid collaboration. The costs and benefits are real for both.

The best version, I think, is some kind of hybrid. The flexibility and focus of remote work, but with some regular in-person time. The structure and collaboration of office work, but not every single day. Unfortunately, hybrid work is hard to get right. It tends to create a two-tier system where remote workers feel like they're missing out and office workers feel like they're doing more than their share.

I also learned something about the nature of productivity. Working from home, I could measure productivity in terms of focused work hours—time spent on tasks that required concentration. I was more productive by that measure. But there's also a kind of network productivity, the value of being around people who do adjacent work, who see what you're working on, who have ideas or suggestions. That's harder to measure, but it's real.

At the end of the year, when I had the option to continue remote or come back to an office, I chose to go back. Not because remote work was bad, but because I'd learned what I get from being in a shared space, and I missed it. I also learned what I'd gained from remote work, and I didn't want to lose it entirely.

What the year taught me is that the question of "is remote work better?" is less important than understanding what you gain and what you lose. The answer depends on what kind of work you do, how you prefer to structure your time, and how much you value various kinds of human connection. For me, the answer turned out to be that I need both—the focus of remote work and the connection of being present in a shared space. But the balance is different for everyone.
