# Heating Season

My woodstove arrived on a Wednesday in October, delivered by a man who studied my setup and said, "You sure about this?" and I said yes, not mentioning that I wasn't sure about anything. He installed the chimney, walked me through the operation once, and left me alone with a cast iron box that would, if I did this right, keep me warm for the next six months.

The first real cold night, I built my first fire. The kindling caught, the logs caught, and I stood there watching the flames, aware that I was about to either sleep comfortably or learn a hard lesson about poor preparation. The stove worked. Heat poured out into the room.

By January, I understood that heating with wood requires a kind of constant negotiation with the weather. On mild days, you don't need the fire at all, so you let it die down to coals and let the house cool slowly. On very cold nights, you load it completely before bed, pushing logs in until they're packed tightly enough to burn slowly through the dark hours. You learn how long different wood burns, how tight to pack it, how to adjust the air vents to control the speed of combustion. Too much air and everything burns in four hours. Too little and the fire smolders, producing smoke and creosote without enough heat.

The house develops a temperature geography. The room with the stove is always warm. Rooms farther away stay cooler. I adapt by spending more time near the heat source—reading by the stove, cooking there when possible. The thermometer rarely goes above 65 degrees Fahrenheit in the rest of the house. But right next to the stove, it's sometimes too warm to sit.

The wood itself becomes a project. I buy a cord in the fall, stacked in my yard. But this wood needs to be seasoned. Green wood—wood that was cut recently—has too much moisture and won't burn well. The wood needs to have dried for at least six months, preferably a year. So I buy this year's wood for next year's fire. There's a relationship between your past, present, and future that heating with wood makes unavoidable.

Ashes accumulate. Every few days, I clean the stove, removing the accumulated ash and depositing it in a bucket. This ash is useful—it can go in the garden or on icy paths—but it also needs to be managed. Some ashes go back in the stove as a bed for the next fire; they insulate the logs and help the fire burn more completely.

There's a satisfaction to this work that I didn't expect. Each day, I'm making a decision about heat. Each load of wood is a small investment in the next few hours of warmth. On cold nights, when the house is silent and warm, I feel the presence of the wood I cut or stacked or paid for months earlier. The heat is immediate, physical, and entirely dependent on my labor.

This kind of heating is not efficient by modern standards. A forced-air oil furnace would be easier. But it would also disconnect me from the process. I wouldn't see the fuel arriving or be aware of how much I was burning. I wouldn't have the continuous negotiation with weather. The house would simply reach a set temperature and stay there.

By spring, when the weather finally stays warm and I let the last fire die down to ash, I understand that something has shifted. I've spent six months in a slow dance with heat and cold. I've learned the properties of different woods, the sounds the stove makes when it's running right, the exact light of coals when you're about to load it for the night. I've stopped thinking about heating as an invisible system and started experiencing it as a daily practice.

When the true warm weather comes and I close the damper for the season, it feels like the end of something. But not quite an ending. Because in the back of my mind, I'm already thinking about next year, about the wood that needs to dry, about the first fire I'll build come October.
