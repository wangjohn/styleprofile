# Traveling by Train

I boarded the train in Chicago at eight in the morning, and for the next forty-five hours, I barely left it. The journey crossed through the northern United States—Chicago to Milwaukee to Minneapolis and then westward through Minnesota and the Dakotas into Montana. It was a route I'd never traveled, a landscape that would be mostly unfamiliar until I arrived. I had a sleeper cabin, a book, and nowhere I needed to be until arrival.

Traveling by train is fundamentally different from other forms of transportation. You're not locked into the pressure of speed the way you are on a plane, where the entire experience is optimized around moving as quickly as possible. You're not isolated in a car the way you are driving. You're in a kind of social space, a moving community of strangers who are all going somewhere.

The cabin was small—a bed, a window, a seat that converted to part of the bed, a washing sink. Everything was functional and compact. The window faced the direction of travel, which meant I could watch the landscape passing. The first day, it was the industrial landscape around Chicago, gradually shifting to farmland as we moved north. The details were hypnotic—individual buildings, the patterns of fields, the way rivers curved. Watching a landscape change gradually rather than suddenly reveals something that faster travel obscures.

The dining car was where the social life of the train concentrated. Meals were served at communal tables, and you were seated with other passengers. You couldn't choose; the staff would seat you with people heading to different destinations, from different places, with different reasons for being on the train. The forced intimacy was awkward at first—eating with strangers, making small talk about where you were headed and why.

But something shifted after an hour. The shared experience of traveling became the shared experience of living in the same space for the duration. A couple from Quebec were traveling to visit family in Seattle. A man from Denver was returning home after a business trip. A woman was traveling alone, as I was, moving across the country. We had all chosen to spend 45 hours on this train, which was an unusual choice, and that itself became a point of connection.

The conversations were better than most travel encounters because there was time. You're not rushing between connections or racing against a clock. You have hours. People relax. The conversation can meander, can follow tangents, can actually develop into something beyond the standard "where are you from, where are you going" exchange.

Between meals, I read in my cabin or sat in the observation car—a car with windows on all sides, designed specifically for watching the landscape pass. This is where I spent most of my waking time. The observation car was full of other people doing the same thing. Nobody talked much, but there was a companionable silence, everyone engaged with the passing world.

The landscape shifted as the train moved west. The Midwest gave way to more open country. Waterways became prominent. Around Minneapolis, there was the Mississippi River, smaller than I expected at this point in its journey but immediately recognizable. As we moved into South Dakota and North Dakota, the landscape became flatter, more wheat fields, wider horizons. There was a point somewhere around Grand Forks where I realized I was no longer seeing trees as regular features of the landscape.

Sleeping on the train was strange. The rhythm of the wheels on the track created a constant vibration, a low-frequency hum that you felt rather than heard. There was something soothing about it once you stopped fighting it. The darkness outside the window was absolute except when the train passed through towns. The occasional jolt as the train took a switch, moved between sections of track. It was not comfortable sleeping—the motion and the newness of the environment prevented that—but it was restful in a way.

By the second day, the novelty had worn off and I settled into a kind of timelessness. The train moved at its own pace, which was neither fast nor slow but something in between. Meals happened on schedule. The landscape changed gradually. I read when I wanted to, watched when I wanted to, slept in chunks rather than in one consolidated block. The rhythm was unlike any other travel I'd experienced.

Montana revealed itself as we moved westward. The landscape opened up further. Mountains appeared in the distance, then became more prominent as we approached them. The North Fork of the Flathead River ran alongside the tracks for part of the journey, water that was clear enough to see the bottom in some sections.

By the final hours, the mountains were directly around us. We wound through valleys, past small towns that existed only because the railroad was there. The train slowed even more as we approached the end of the line. The passengers who were getting off began gathering their things, moving to the stairs, preparing for the transition from train to ground.

When I stepped off the train in Whitefish, Montana, the world seemed very still. The ground was solid in a way I'd forgotten about. The air was sharper, colder, scented with pine. After 45 hours of movement, the stillness was disorienting.

But I carried something from the journey. The forty-five hours had created distance from whatever was before. The train had been a transition, not just a way to get from one place to another but an actual space between my old life and the next thing. By the time I arrived, I was already someone else than I had been.

That's what traveling by train offers that faster transportation cannot: the time to actually change, to actually move from one place to another rather than just appearing somewhere new. It's inefficient. But sometimes the inefficiency is the point.
