# Sticking to a Budget

Most budget advice assumes that budgeting is about willpower—that if you just force yourself to stick to a number, everything will work out. This assumes the problem is that you're spending too much on frivolous things. But the real problem is usually structural. The spending isn't frivolous—it's necessary. It's just spread across enough categories that you don't have a clear picture of where it all goes.

Here's a practical approach that doesn't rely on willpower so much as visibility and friction.

**First, separate what's fixed from what's variable.** Fixed costs are the things that don't change: rent, insurance, utilities, loan payments. You can't really reduce these without making major changes to your life, so don't bother trying. Accept that these are your baseline. Variable costs are everything else. Those are the place to focus.

**Track everything for a month.** Every coffee, every grocery trip, every subscription you forgot about. Use a spreadsheet, a notebook, or an app. The tracking is not about judgment—it's about creating a picture of where your money actually goes. This is almost always surprising. People think their biggest expense is one category when it's actually something else entirely.

**Look for the categories where small decisions add up.** Coffee every weekday is seventy dollars a month. The takeout lunch habit is three hundred. The subscription services you signed up for and forgot about are perhaps sixty. These aren't morality problems. They're just leaks in different places. Identifying them is the hard part. Fixing them is usually straightforward once you know what they are.

**Make the default the thing you want to do.** If you want to bring lunch from home but you're buying lunch because you're tired and forgot, the problem isn't your willpower. It's that the default is to buy lunch. Change the default. Cook extra dinner so lunch is there waiting for you. It takes less willpower to eat something that's already made than to cook something new.

**For discretionary spending, create friction in the direction you want to go.** If you want to spend less on eating out, don't keep restaurant apps on your phone. Make cooking at home easy by keeping staples stocked. If you want to spend less on something, make the spending harder—not impossible, but harder than the alternative.

**Build in actual discretionary spending.** The budgets that fail are the ones where every dollar is accounted for. You're human. You're going to want something that's not in the budget. If there's no room for it, you'll either blow the budget entirely or feel resentful the whole time. Instead, plan for discretionary spending. Give yourself a category and a limit. Maybe it's fifty dollars a month for whatever you want. Once you hit fifty, you're done for the month. But you knew about it and planned for it.

**Account for irregular expenses.** The budget that fails is the one that doesn't include car repairs and dental work and replacing the water heater. These don't happen every month, but they happen regularly. Budget for them by setting aside money each month, even in months when the expense doesn't actually occur. Then when it does occur, you have the money available.

**Save before spending.** This is the single most effective change most people can make. Instead of saving whatever is left after spending, spend whatever is left after saving. Move money to savings first—automatic transfer is ideal—and budget with what remains. You'll spend what's available, and you'll save what you designated.

**Adjust based on reality.** Your first budget will be wrong. That's fine. Track your actual spending for a month and see where your estimates were off. Adjust. Do this a few times until your budget roughly matches your actual spending. Now the budget is a useful tool rather than a fantasy about how you wish you would spend money.

The goal isn't to never spend on anything enjoyable. The goal is to understand where your money goes and make intentional choices about it. Once you have that visibility, sticking to a budget becomes much easier because you're not relying on willpower. You're relying on structure.
