# What a Garden Teaches

I started a vegetable garden without any clear plan. I had a sunny space, I liked the idea of eating things I'd grown, and I had time in the spring to dig. I bought seeds, prepared the soil with a beginner's enthusiasm, and planted. Within a few weeks, I realized I was trying to learn biology through failure.

The first lesson was about attention. The plants needed water, and they needed it at the right times. The wrong time was obvious—plants that dried out completely often didn't recover. But the right time was less obvious. Watering too often encouraged root rot. Not watering enough meant stunted growth. Different plants had different needs. I developed a habit of checking the soil every morning, understanding that consistency mattered more than intensity.

The second lesson was about patience. Everything grew on its own timeline, not the timeline I wanted. The tomatoes took months to set fruit, and then months more to ripen. The lettuce grew much faster, ready to pick in weeks. But if you harvested it all at once, you had lettuce. If you harvested gradually, you had a continuous supply. The garden taught me to work with natural rhythms rather than trying to impose my own.

The third lesson was about loss. Some plants didn't make it. The squash plants got powdery mildew and stopped producing. One row of beans got some disease and had to be pulled up. A section of carrots simply failed to thrive, maybe the soil was wrong, maybe something was eating them underground. The loss was real but not catastrophic. There was always something else growing. The goal wasn't perfection; the goal was production.

The fourth lesson was harder: about competition. I had planted everything densely, and in some sections the plants were competing with each other for water, nutrients, light. The ones that got crowded didn't thrive. I learned, too late for some plantings, that spacing mattered as much as soil quality. I started removing plants even though they were healthy, just to give the neighbors room. It was a form of cultivation that involved actively removing potential.

By mid-summer, the garden was producing. We had tomatoes, beans, lettuce, basil, peppers, squash. The abundance was unexpected. One zucchini plant could produce more than two people could eat. The lettuce that we harvested regularly just kept producing new leaves. The tomatoes ripened continuously. We were eating vegetables from the garden every day.

But we weren't eating the tomatoes we'd planted because they were beautiful or perfectly formed. We were eating them because they were there. Some were misshapen. Some had cracks. Some were smaller than store-bought equivalents. But they tasted like tomatoes in a way that others didn't, and they were free in the sense that we'd just put in labor and attention and received food in return.

By fall, as the days got shorter and colder, production slowed. We harvested the last tomatoes before the first frost. We pulled up the dead plants and composted them. I put the beds to bed for winter, spreading mulch, leaving some cover crops that would enrich the soil for next year.

What the garden taught me is that production is not about control. It's about working with constraints, respecting natural processes, accepting loss, and being attentive. You can't force a plant to grow. You can only create the conditions for growth and pay attention to whether those conditions are right.

And once you've grown something, everything changes. Vegetables taste different when you've tended them. They matter differently. You understand, in a way you don't from the grocery store, that food is the product of attention, of time, of working with something that has its own life. You can't hurry it. You can only be present while it grows.
