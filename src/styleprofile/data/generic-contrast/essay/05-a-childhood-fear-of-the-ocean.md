# What the Water Held

When I was six, a wave knocked me down at a beach in North Carolina and dragged me along the sand for what felt like an hour and was probably four seconds. I came up coughing, with a mouthful of salt and a bathing suit full of grit. My mother scooped me up, laughing with relief. I did not laugh. I had learned something that no one had warned me about: the ocean was not a big pool. It was a living thing, and it did not care about me.

For the next ten years I kept my distance. Family trips to the coast became exercises in negotiation. I would sit on a towel well above the tide line, building elaborate sand fortresses while my cousins shrieked and splashed in the surf. If someone coaxed me to the edge, I would let the foam touch my ankles and then retreat, heart pounding. What frightened me was not drowning exactly. It was the not-knowing. Under the surface was a darkness I could not read, and the waves arrived without any pattern I could predict.

Adults tried to reason with me. My uncle explained rip currents with diagrams drawn in the sand. A lifeguard once showed me how to float on my back. These lessons helped my head but not my stomach. Fear does not take direction well.

What finally shifted things was a small thing. At sixteen I went on a trip with a friend's family, and her little brother, who was five, simply took my hand and said, "Come see the crabs." He wasn't brave, he was just curious. We waded in up to our knees, and I watched tiny crabs scuttle through the shallows, and I realized I had been so busy bracing for the water that I had never looked at it.

I would like to say I became a swimmer that afternoon. I did not. But I learned to stay, to watch the pattern of the swells, to notice how they gathered and broke. Attention turned out to be a kind of courage.

I swim in the ocean now, carefully, with respect. Last summer I taught my own son to duck under a wave. I told him it would push him, and that this was fine. Then we stood in the water together and watched it come.
