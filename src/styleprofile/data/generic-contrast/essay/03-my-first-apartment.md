# My First Apartment

The apartment was on the third floor of a converted house on Alder Street, and it cost four hundred and eighty dollars a month. I signed the lease at twenty-two with a pen that kept skipping, and the landlord, a retired plumber named Mr. Kowalski, watched me do it with an expression somewhere between pity and amusement.

It had one real room, a kitchenette along one wall, and a bathroom so narrow that I could touch both sides with my elbows. The radiator clanked all winter like someone trapped in the pipes. The window over the sink looked out at a brick wall, though if I leaned to the left I could see a slice of sky. I thought it was the most beautiful place in the world.

My furniture came from the curb and from my aunt's basement. I owned a mattress on the floor for the first two months, a lamp with no shade, and a card table I used for eating, working, and occasionally playing solitaire at two in the morning. I learned that a single pot can cook almost anything if you are hungry enough. I learned to cut my own hair, badly. I learned that rent is due on the first whether or not the week has been kind to you.

What I remember most, though, is the quiet. Growing up in a house of five, I had never been truly alone. In that apartment, silence had a texture. It pressed on my ears the first night, and I turned on the radio just to hear another voice. By the end of the month I was turning it off on purpose.

There were lonely evenings. I called my mother more often than I admitted, and I once ate cereal for dinner four nights running because I had miscalculated my budget. But there were also small victories that belonged only to me: the first time I fixed the leaky faucet with a wrench borrowed from Mr. Kowalski, the first dinner party with four mismatched chairs, the first morning I woke up and realized I had stopped feeling like a guest.

I moved out after three years, and I have lived in nicer places since. Still, when I drive past Alder Street, I slow down and look up at the third-floor window. Somebody else's curtains hang there now. I hope they are happy, and I hope the radiator still clanks.
