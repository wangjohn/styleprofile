The alarm went off at 3:40 every morning that summer, and for the first week I hated it with a purity I had not known I was capable of. I was nineteen, home from college, and my mother had arranged the job through a friend who owned Hartmann's Bakery on the corner of Delancey and Fifth. "It builds character," she said. I said character was overrated. She handed me an apron.

Hartmann's was a small place that smelled so strongly of butter and yeast that the scent clung to my hair even after I washed it twice. The owner, Gerda Hartmann, was in her sixties, built like a fireplug, with forearms that could knead forty pounds of dough without a visible effort. She did not believe in small talk before six a.m. On my first morning she looked at me, looked at the clock, and pointed at a fifty-pound sack of flour. That was my orientation.

The work was physical in a way my classes never had been. I scaled ingredients, hauled trays, shaped rolls, and cleaned. Always, there was cleaning. Gerda held that a bakery was a kitchen first and a business second, and she could detect a film of flour on a counter from across the room. I learned to wipe in long strokes, to wash the bench scraper the moment I finished with it, to keep my station so tidy that, as she put it, "a surgeon could eat off it."

Slowly my body adjusted. The 3:40 alarm became ordinary. My shoulders broadened. I discovered that the hours between four and seven in the morning, before the shop opened, held a peculiar calm. Radio low, ovens ticking as they heated, the city outside still dark. Gerda and I worked side by side with the shared efficiency of people who don't need to speak. Occasionally she'd hand me a warm roll from the first batch and watch while I ate it. "Well?" she would say. "Good," I'd answer, and she'd nod as though I had passed an exam.

I did not pass every exam. In July I ruined an entire batch of croissant dough by letting the butter get too warm, and I stood before forty wasted pounds of greasy, sullen pastry feeling that I had personally failed civilization. I waited for her to yell. She came over, poked the dough, sighed, and said, "Butter is like people. It does not like being rushed." Then she made me start again. The second batch was better. The third was good enough to sell.

The customers became part of the texture of those weeks. There was Mr. Delgado, who came every morning at 6:15 for a single plain bagel and a long discussion of baseball. There was a young nurse coming off the night shift who always looked as though she might cry, and who always bought a cinnamon twist, and whom I started to save the best one for. There was a toddler who pointed at the pastry case and said "dat" with a persistence that deserved a medal. I discovered that feeding people is a form of intimacy. You see them at their most unguarded: tired, hungry, hopeful.

Money was part of it, of course. I earned a little over minimum wage and paid for a good portion of the next semester's books. But the summer's real wage was harder to name. I learned to show up when I didn't feel like it. I learned that a craft requires repetition before it requires inspiration, and that pride can live in something as modest as a perfectly even row of dinner rolls.

On my last day, Gerda gave me a paper bag. Inside were half a dozen rolls and a small, battered bench scraper, its wooden handle worn smooth. "That one's mine," she said. "Don't lose it." It was the longest speech she'd made all summer, and I had to look at the ceiling for a moment.

I never worked in a bakery again. I went back to school, then into an office, and then into another office. But I still bake bread on Sunday mornings, and the scraper sits in a drawer in my kitchen. When the dough comes together under my hands, I can smell Hartmann's, hear the ovens ticking, and see Gerda nodding, as though I had passed.
