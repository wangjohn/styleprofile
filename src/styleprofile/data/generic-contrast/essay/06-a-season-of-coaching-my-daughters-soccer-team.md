# Eleven Girls and a Bag of Orange Slices

Nobody else volunteered. That is the honest origin of my season as a soccer coach. At the parents' meeting in August, the league coordinator asked three times whether anyone would lead the under-eight Fireflies, and each time the room studied its shoes. My daughter Maya, who was seven and had been talking about soccer since June, looked at me from across the gym. I raised my hand before I had decided to.

I should explain that I knew almost nothing about the sport. I had played a bit of kickball. I owned no whistle. The league sent me a laminated packet of drills with names like "Sharks and Minnows," and I studied it the way I once studied for the bar exam, highlighting, tabbing, making flash cards. Maya found the flash cards and asked if I was okay.

Our first practice was a catastrophe, though a cheerful one. Eleven girls arrived in eleven different states of readiness. One had her shin guards on the outside of her socks. One cried because her sister was on a different team. Two spent the entire hour examining a dandelion. I blew my brand-new whistle and nothing happened, so I shouted, and nothing happened to that either. Then a girl named Priya tugged my sleeve and said, very kindly, "Coach, can we just kick the ball?"

We kicked the ball. That became the central strategy of the season.

What I learned over the following weeks was that seven-year-olds are not small adults and do not respond to adult logic. Telling them to "spread out" produced a tight swarm that followed the ball like bees around a jar. Explaining positions was useless; they all wanted to be the striker, and, more precisely, they all wanted to be wherever the ball was. So I stopped explaining. I made up games instead. In one, they had to dribble through a field of cones while I played a hungry crocodile. In another, the goal was to steal my socks. Attendance went up, and so, slowly, did something that resembled skill.

There was a Saturday in October that I think about often. We were losing four to nothing against a team that had clearly been practicing since the womb. The girls were drooping. At halftime I had nothing clever to say, so I handed out orange slices and asked who had seen the best thing so far. Everyone had an answer. Someone had seen a butterfly. Someone had seen a goalie dive into a puddle. Maya said her favorite part was when Priya fell down and then popped up laughing.

In the second half, we lost six to nothing. Nobody minded.

I do not want to make this sound like pure sweetness, because it wasn't. There were parents who yelled from the sideline, including, once, me, in a moment I regret. A father of one of our players questioned my substitution pattern with an intensity that seemed disproportionate to a game in which the score was not officially kept. I felt defensive, then embarrassed, then oddly tender toward him, because I recognized the hunger underneath. He wanted his kid to be seen. So did I.

That, I think, was the real lesson. Coaching was less about soccer than about attention. Eleven children needed, in turn, to be noticed: the shy one who never touched the ball, the fast one who was bored, the one who needed to hear that a mistake was not a catastrophe. My daughter needed something different from the rest, and for the first time I had to figure out how to be her coach on Saturday and her father at dinner without letting either role swallow the other. We argued once in the car about her effort. She was right and I apologized. It was probably the most useful thing I did all year.

By November, something had changed. In our last game the Fireflies strung together three passes in a row, a thing I had not believed possible in September. Priya scored from an awkward angle, off her knee, and the whole sideline erupted. She looked stunned, then furious that no one had told her she could do that, then radiant.

We finished with a record of one win, one tie, and six losses. At the end-of-season party, each girl received a plastic trophy with a gold figure kicking a ball, and I gave each a short speech I had written the night before. Mostly I got through it. When I reached Maya, my voice wobbled, and she rolled her eyes, but she hugged me hard in front of everyone.

I am coaching again in the spring. I now own a whistle I rarely use, and I have a mesh bag for the orange slices. I have also, finally, learned what the girls knew from the beginning: sometimes the point is just to kick the ball.
