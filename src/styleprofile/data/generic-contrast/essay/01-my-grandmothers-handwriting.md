# My Grandmother's Handwriting

I found her recipe box last spring while cleaning out a cabinet, and the first card I pulled out stopped me cold. There it was: the slanted, looping script I had known since childhood, every capital letter dressed up with a small flourish, every "t" crossed with a long stroke that ran well past the stem.

My grandmother wrote the way she spoke, unhurried and a little formal. She learned penmanship in a one-room school, where girls practiced ovals and push-pulls until their wrists ached. You could see that training in everything she touched. Birthday cards, grocery lists, the labels she stuck on jars of peach preserves. Even a note saying she had gone to the neighbor's was composed with care, as though someone might frame it.

As a kid I found it hard to read. I would hold her letters up to the light and squint at the cursive, asking her what a word said, and she would sigh and tell me. Now I would give almost anything for one more confusing note.

I held the recipe card for a long time. A smudge of butter had gone translucent in one corner. Her instructions for pie crust ended with the words "Don't overwork it, dear." I read that line aloud in her voice, and for a moment the kitchen felt warmer.

I have tried to copy her loops since then. My hand is cramped and impatient, and the results look like a forgery done by a nervous stranger. Still, I keep practicing.
