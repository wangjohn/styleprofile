# Raising a Glass to Daniel

When my brother asked me to give the toast at his wedding, I said yes immediately and then spent the next four months in a low panic. I am the older sibling, the one who is supposed to have something wise to say. But Daniel has always had a way of making wisdom feel beside the point, and I couldn't figure out how to talk about him without either embarrassing him or lying.

I wrote nine drafts. The first was a list of funny stories, which my wife read and diplomatically described as "a roast." The second was heartfelt but sounded like a greeting card. The third quoted a poet I had never actually read. By the sixth, I understood that I was avoiding the real subject, which was how strange it was to watch the kid I once pushed off a swing set stand under an arch of flowers and promise himself to someone else.

So let me describe what I eventually said, and what I wanted to say.

I began with the swing set. When I was ten and Daniel was six, I pushed him off one in a fit of sibling rage, and he broke his wrist. I expected to be punished severely. What actually happened is that Daniel, in his cast, told our parents he had fallen by himself. He kept the story up for years. I learned much later that he had known exactly what happened. He simply decided, at six, that I was worth protecting. The room laughed at this, and I saw Daniel put his face in his hands, but I also saw his eyes shining.

That, I told them, is who my brother is. He is generous in ways that don't announce themselves. He is the person who shows up at your door with a toolbox when you mention a dripping faucet. He remembers your mother's birthday. He listens with his whole face, and he asks the second question, the one that shows he was actually paying attention.

Then I turned to Elena, his bride, and this was the part I had rewritten most. I didn't want to flatter her, because she would see through it in a second. Elena is sharp and quick, a woman who corrects my grammar with affection, and she makes my brother braver. Before her, Daniel was kind but cautious. He kept his options open. With Elena, I told the guests, I watched him become decisive. The man who once took forty minutes to order a sandwich asked her to marry him in an afternoon.

More laughter, and Elena bowed her head, grinning.

I wanted to say something true about marriage, though I confess I am no expert. Mine has lasted eleven years, which means I know just enough to be humble. What I have observed is that the best marriages seem to be ongoing acts of translation. Two people, raised in different houses with different unspoken rules, keep learning to read each other. It's not glamorous. It involves a great deal of patience, and an even greater supply of forgiveness for small things: the dishes, the thermostat, the way one of you hums while the other is trying to concentrate. And the miracle, if there is one, is that after enough years you find you have built a private language no one else speaks.

I looked at my brother then and said what I had been holding back all evening. That I was proud of him. That our father, who couldn't be with us, would have been prouder, and would have pretended otherwise by making a bad joke about the open bar. At that, the room went soft and quiet, and several people wiped their eyes, including me, and including a groomsman who later denied it.

I finished by asking everyone to stand. I told them to lift their glasses to two people who had chosen each other on purpose, in full view of all of us, which is a brave thing to do. To Daniel and Elena: may your house always be loud with laughter, may your arguments be short, and may you always protect each other, even when it isn't strictly necessary.

Glasses clinked. Daniel crossed the floor and hugged me hard enough to bruise a rib. "You didn't have to tell the swing set story," he whispered. "I know," I said. "I wanted to."

I still have the ninth draft, folded in my wallet, soft and creased now. I never actually read from it. At the last moment I put it in my pocket and spoke without notes, which is perhaps the best reason to have written so many.
