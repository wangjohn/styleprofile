# The Four-Day Workweek Deserves a Trial

The five-day week feels like a law of nature, but it is a habit. It took decades of argument, and plenty of predictions of ruin, to move from six days to five. Nobody serious now wants to go back. It is fair to ask whether the next step is overdue.

The evidence from pilots is encouraging. Trials in Britain, Iceland, Portugal and elsewhere have asked employers to cut hours to about thirty-two a week with no loss of pay. Most participating firms reported steady or improved output, fewer sick days and lower turnover. Workers said they felt less burned out and more able to manage family, health and errands. A large share of companies chose to keep the schedule after the trial ended.

Skeptics raise fair points. Some jobs, such as hospitals, shops and emergency services, cannot simply close for an extra day. Some pilots involved small, flexible firms that were already well run. And a compressed week of four ten-hour days is not the same as genuinely fewer hours.

But those are arguments for careful design, not for refusing to look. A trial is cheap, reversible and measurable. A company can set clear targets, run the experiment for six months, and go back if it fails. Governments can encourage this by offering guidance and sharing results, as some already do.

There is also a quieter reason to try. Much of the working day is eaten by meetings that could have been emails and by tasks stretched to fill the time. A shorter week forces an honest look at what is essential. People concentrate more when they know the clock is tighter.

None of this promises that every organization will benefit. It simply says that a century-old assumption should have to earn its place. If a trial shows that people can do as well in four days, we will have found a free improvement in how we live. If it does not, we lose very little.

The question is not whether we can afford to try. It is whether we can afford never to find out.
