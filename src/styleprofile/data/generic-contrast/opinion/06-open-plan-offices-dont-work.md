# Open-Plan Offices Don't Work

Every few years, a company announces that it is tearing down its walls. The press release promises collaboration, creativity and a flatter culture. Executives pose in front of rows of shared desks, and architects explain that ideas will now flow freely through the space. A year later, the employees are wearing headphones.

The open-plan office is one of the great management fads of the modern era, and the evidence against it has been accumulating for years. It is time to say plainly that it does not do what it was built to do.

Start with the central promise, that removing barriers makes people talk to each other more. When researchers at Harvard studied two companies that moved from traditional offices to open layouts, they found the opposite. Face-to-face interaction fell by about seventy percent, while email and messaging rose. People, it turns out, do not like to have conversations in front of an audience. They retreat into their screens, type quietly and avoid eye contact. The open office did not create a village square. It created a library where no one is allowed to say anything interesting.

Then there is the noise. Concentrated work, writing, analysis, coding, careful editing, depends on sustained attention, and human speech is among the most disruptive sounds there is. A conversation nearby, even one you are not trying to follow, drains the mental resources you need for your own task. Studies of office workers consistently find that noise and interruption are the top complaints, and that performance on complex tasks suffers. Every interruption also carries a recovery cost: it takes many minutes to return to deep focus after being pulled out of it. In a space where interruptions are constant, deep focus becomes a rare event.

Health is another casualty. Employees in open offices report more stress and take more sick days than those with private or semi-private spaces. This should not surprise anyone who has shared a room with thirty colleagues during flu season. Add the lack of control over temperature, light and air, and the lack of any place to take a difficult phone call, and the office becomes a daily test of endurance.

Privacy matters too, and not only for sensitive work. People need somewhere to make a medical appointment, to have a hard conversation with a manager, to cry for a minute after bad news, or simply to think without being watched. Workers in open plans often describe the feeling of constant surveillance, aware that anyone can see their screen and judge how busy they look. That performance of productivity is its own kind of waste. People stay late, or sit stiffly at their desks, to appear diligent rather than to do good work.

Why, then, do companies keep building these spaces? The honest answer is money. Open plans pack more people into fewer square feet. Rent is one of the largest costs a business faces, and cutting the space per employee produces a number that looks good on a spreadsheet. The collaboration language is a justification that arrived afterward. When a firm says it wants openness, it often means it wants to save on real estate, and the savings are real even if the productivity losses are harder to measure.

Defenders of the open plan are right about one thing: collaboration matters, and walls can isolate people. Nobody wants to return to a world of closed doors and long corridors where a team never meets. But the choice is not between a cubicle farm and a cavernous room. The better answer is variety. Offices work when they offer a mix: quiet rooms for focused work, small meeting spaces for conversation, comfortable common areas for informal contact, and desks that people can personalize. Let employees choose where to work depending on the task in front of them.

The pandemic taught many people that they could concentrate better at home, and it has left them less willing to return to a noisy floor. Employers who want staff back in the building should treat that as a message. If the office is worse than the spare bedroom for doing your job, no amount of free snacks will bring people back willingly.

None of this requires a grand redesign. Companies can start by asking workers what they need, rather than assuming. They can add partitions, build a few enclosed rooms, set norms about quiet hours and put an end to the expectation that being seen is the same as working. Some will find that giving people doors costs less than losing them.

The open-plan office was sold as a way to bring people together. It has mostly driven them apart, behind noise-cancelling headphones and chat windows. We can still build workplaces that serve both focus and connection. First we have to admit that the fashionable experiment has failed.
