# The Case for Rewilding Lawns

The American lawn covers an area larger than any irrigated crop in the country, by some estimates around forty million acres. We water it, mow it, fertilize it and spray it, and in return we get a monoculture of grass that feeds almost nothing, shelters almost nothing and costs us hours of every summer weekend. It is hard to think of a more elaborate way of producing so little.

I say this as someone who grew up admiring a good lawn. My father edged his with a spade and stood back to look at it the way other men look at a car. The lawn was a sign of care, of belonging to a neighborhood, of having your life in order. I understand its pull. But the pull is cultural, not natural, and it is time to ask whether the habit still deserves our land.

Start with what a lawn is. The turf grasses on most yards are not native to the places where they grow. They were chosen for uniform color and tolerance of mowing, and they stay green through dry months only with heavy watering. In many cities, a large share of summer household water goes onto the grass. That is drinking-quality water, treated and pumped at public expense, poured onto ground that will return nothing edible. In drought-prone regions, the arithmetic has become indefensible, and some water districts now pay homeowners to rip up turf.

Then there is the work and pollution of upkeep. Gas-powered mowers, trimmers and blowers emit far more pollutants per hour than a car, and the noise carries across every fence. Fertilizer and herbicide wash into storm drains and eventually into streams and bays, feeding algae blooms and harming aquatic life. The average suburban yard receives more chemicals per acre than much farmland. We apply them to keep a patch of ground looking like a golf course, which is not a goal that survives scrutiny.

The cost to wildlife is the part that moves me most. Insects are in steep decline in many parts of the world, and with them go the birds, bats and amphibians that depend on them. Lawn grass supports few insect species. Native plants, by contrast, have evolved alongside local pollinators, caterpillars and beetles, and a single oak can host hundreds of species of caterpillars. Those caterpillars are what songbirds feed their nestlings, and a chickadee needs thousands of them to raise a single brood. A yard planted in turf offers the chicks nothing. A yard with native trees, shrubs and flowers becomes a small piece of working habitat.

Individually, a yard is small. Together, they are enormous. The ecologist Doug Tallamy has argued that private land is the great unused resource of conservation: there are far more acres in yards than in national parks in the lower forty-eight states, and if homeowners converted even half their lawns to native plantings, they would knit together a network of habitat that no government program could afford to build. That is a hopeful idea, because it does not depend on legislation or on anyone's permission. It depends on a shovel and a willingness to let a patch of ground become a little untidy.

Untidiness, of course, is where the resistance begins. Neighbors complain. Homeowners' associations send letters. Municipal codes in many towns cap vegetation at six or eight inches, and a few have fined residents for gardens that looked wild to an inspector. These rules grew out of reasonable worries about rats, fire and falling property values, but they have been applied far too broadly, and they treat a meadow the same as an abandoned lot. A well-planned native garden, with a mown border, clear paths and a small sign explaining what it is, looks deliberate because it is. Several states have passed laws limiting the power of associations to ban such gardens, and more should follow.

Critics raise other objections. Won't a rewilded yard attract ticks and mosquitoes? Ticks favor the edges between lawn and woods and the damp leaf litter, but they thrive in unmanaged suburban lawns just as well, and a diverse planting tends to support the predators that keep pest populations in check. Mosquitoes breed in standing water, which a lawn offers as readily as a garden does. What about children and dogs? They need somewhere to play, and nobody is arguing for a yard with no open space. A small patch of mown grass, perhaps a third of the yard, for ball games and picnics, is entirely compatible with a border of native planting and a pollinator bed.

The most common objection is simply effort. Replacing a lawn sounds like a huge project. It need not be. You can begin with one corner, a strip along the fence, or the area beneath a tree where grass struggles anyway. Cut the turf or smother it under cardboard and mulch, plant a handful of regionally appropriate species, and water them through the first season. After that, the yard mostly takes care of itself. The work you save on mowing and watering will exceed what you put in within a couple of years. Many people discover that gardening with native plants is more interesting than mowing, because something is always changing: a bloom opens, a butterfly arrives, a bird you have never seen stops to feed on seed heads.

There is an aesthetic argument too, one that is easy to lose among the ecological ones. A lawn is static. It is the same green in April and in August, in the morning and in the evening. A meadow garden shifts constantly, with spring ephemerals, summer asters, autumn grasses turning copper and winter seed heads dusted with frost. It has texture, motion and the sound of insects. It tells you where you are, in a way that a patch of imported turf cannot. Landscapes that reflect their region are beautiful because they belong.

None of this means we must abolish every lawn tomorrow. Grass has its uses: it is a soft surface for play, it cools the ground, it keeps mud at bay. The target is the reflex of treating lawn as the default, as though nothing else could possibly go in front of a house. Make it a choice, and a smaller one. Plant a tree. Let the clover come in. Skip the chemicals. Mow less often, and leave the clippings.

If enough people do this, the change will be visible from the air, a patchwork of green and gold where there was once a uniform carpet. Neighbors will copy neighbors, as they always have. The lawn took over America by imitation, and imitation can undo it. The next time you reach for the mower, look at the ground and ask what else it might hold.
