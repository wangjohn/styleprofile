# Why We Should Stop Treating Sleep as Optional

Ask people how they are, and a surprising number answer with a boast about how little they slept. Five hours, four, none at all before a deadline. The tone is rueful but proud, as though exhaustion were a credential. In offices, hospitals, newsrooms and college dorms, running on fumes has become a badge of seriousness, and sleep a weakness that the committed learn to overcome.

This is a cultural mistake, and an expensive one. Sleep is not a luxury or a habit to be optimized away. It is a biological requirement, as basic as food and water, and a society that treats it as optional pays for the error in health, safety, money and ordinary happiness.

Begin with what sleep does. During the night, the brain consolidates memories, sorting the day's experiences and storing what matters. It clears metabolic waste that accumulates while we are awake. The body repairs tissue, regulates hormones and tunes the immune system. Take away sleep and each of these processes falters. After a single night of short sleep, people have trouble with attention and memory, and their mood sours. Over months and years of shortfall, the effects compound. Chronic sleep loss is associated with higher risks of heart disease, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, obesity, depression and, according to a growing body of research, dementia. Not every link is settled, but the pattern is consistent enough that most sleep scientists regard the evidence as serious.

The popular belief that some people simply need less is mostly a myth. A tiny fraction of the population carries gene variants that allow them to function on about six hours. For nearly everyone else, adults need seven to nine. The trouble is that sleep-deprived people are poor judges of their own impairment. Experiments show that volunteers kept on restricted sleep for two weeks report feeling only slightly tired, while their test scores keep falling. The tired brain loses the ability to notice that it is tired. This is why the person boasting of five hours a night feels fine and performs worse than they realize.

The public consequences are large. Drowsy driving causes a substantial number of road crashes every year, and in some studies the impairment from being awake for twenty hours resembles the effect of a blood alcohol level that would be illegal behind the wheel. Several famous industrial disasters, including the Challenger launch decision, Chernobyl and the Exxon Valdez grounding, have been tied in part to fatigued operators and managers. Medical residents on extended shifts make more errors, a finding that prompted reforms limiting their hours. In the economy at large, researchers have estimated the cost of insufficient sleep in lost productivity at tens or even hundreds of billions of dollars a year in rich countries. A workforce that is chronically tired is a workforce that is making more mistakes and enjoying less of its life.

Why, then, do we keep trimming it? Part of the answer lies in work culture. Long hours signal dedication, and the glorification of the all-nighter lingers in fields that confuse presence with productivity. Part lies in technology. Screens deliver an endless supply of reasons to stay awake, and the blue-tinged light they emit can delay the body's release of melatonin. Streaming services auto-play the next episode before you have decided you want it. Part lies in the structure of our days: commutes, childcare, second jobs and shift schedules leave many people with no good option, and for them the problem is not attitude but arithmetic.

That last point matters, because advice to simply sleep more can be glib. It is easy to tell a person to go to bed earlier. It is harder when the only job available starts at five and ends at midnight, or when a newborn is waking every two hours. A serious approach to sleep must therefore be a social project, not only a personal one.

Schools offer the clearest example. Teenagers undergo a biological shift in their body clocks that makes it natural to fall asleep later and wake later. Yet many high schools start before eight in the morning, forcing students to rise at an hour their bodies treat as the middle of the night. When districts have moved start times later, studies have reported better attendance, higher grades, fewer car accidents and improved mood. The change costs something in bus schedules and after-school sport, but the benefits are large and the objection is mostly administrative. We would not tolerate a medication with such a record of benefit going unprescribed.

Employers can help in similar ways. They can stop rewarding late-night email and make clear that replies can wait until morning. They can set predictable schedules, particularly for shift workers whose rotating hours wreak havoc on the body. They can treat a rested employee as an asset, not as one who is not working hard enough. Some companies have begun to do so, offering nap rooms, flexible hours and training on healthy sleep, and report better retention and fewer errors. Governments can do their part by regulating hours in transport and health care, where fatigue puts others at risk, and by funding sleep research, which has long been neglected relative to its importance.

At the personal level, the advice is dull but sound. Keep a consistent schedule, including weekends. Make the bedroom dark, cool and quiet. Limit caffeine in the afternoon and alcohol in the evening, since alcohol fragments sleep even when it seems to help you doze off. Put the phone out of reach, and give yourself a wind-down period before bed. For persistent insomnia, cognitive behavioral therapy is more effective in the long run than sleeping pills, and it deserves to be more widely available.

Underneath these tips lies a shift in attitude that I think matters most. We should stop describing sleep as time lost and start describing it as part of the work. A chess player does not resent the training that sharpens her game; a surgeon does not consider hand-washing a distraction from surgery. Sleep is the preparation for everything else we do. The student who sleeps well learns more, the manager who sleeps well decides better, the parent who sleeps well is kinder at breakfast.

Nobody has to become obsessive. Occasional bad nights are part of life, and fretting about them can make matters worse. The goal is simply to treat rest as a priority, to defend it when other demands crowd in, and to stop admiring those who sacrifice it. The next time someone proudly announces a night of four hours, try a different response. Ask them how they are really doing, and suggest they go to bed.
