# Tipping Culture Has Gone Too Far

I recently bought a bagel. The cashier turned a tablet toward me, and I found myself choosing between 20, 25 and 30 percent, with a small, grey "no tip" button tucked underneath. Nobody had served me. Nobody had carried anything. I had pointed at a bagel and been handed it.

Tipping began as a reward for exceptional table service. It has since spread to coffee counters, self-checkout kiosks, takeout windows and even the purchase of a single sandwich. The screen asks, a person watches, and the social pressure does the rest. This is not generosity. It is a toll collected through embarrassment.

The deeper problem is what tipping does to the economics of work. When employers pay low wages on the assumption that customers will make up the difference, they shift their costs onto the public while keeping control of the menu price. Diners cannot see what a meal really costs. Workers cannot predict what they will earn. A good server in a busy restaurant may do very well, while a kitchen porter, who earns no tips, watches the gap widen.

Tipping also rewards the wrong things. Research has repeatedly found that tip size correlates more with a server's looks, friendliness to the customer's own demographic and the weather than with the quality of service. It is a lottery wearing the costume of merit.

Customers are tired, and not because they are stingy. They are tired of doing arithmetic at the register, of feeling judged by a stranger, and of being asked to subsidize business models that should stand on their own.

The fix is not complicated. Pay workers a living wage, build it into the price, and let a tip return to what it was meant to be: a small, occasional thank-you for service that went beyond the ordinary. Several restaurants have already abolished tipping and survived. Their menus cost a little more, their staff earn more reliably, and nobody has to feel guilty about a bagel.

Until more businesses follow, the rest of us can do something modest. Tip generously where service is truly personal, decline where it is not, and stop apologizing for pressing that grey button.
