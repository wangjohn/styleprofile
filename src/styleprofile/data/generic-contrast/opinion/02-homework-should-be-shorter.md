# Homework Should Be Shorter

My daughter is nine, and some evenings her homework takes longer than my workday felt at her age. By the time she finishes, dinner is cold, the book she wanted to read stays closed, and she is too tired to talk about anything but how tired she is.

Schools defend homework as practice that builds habits and reinforces learning. For older students, a modest amount probably does. But the research for younger children is thin, and what exists suggests the benefits flatten out quickly. Beyond about an hour for high schoolers, and far less for elementary pupils, extra assignments mostly produce fatigue, not understanding.

Meanwhile the costs are easy to see. Families fight at the kitchen table. Children with less help at home fall behind through no fault of their own, because homework quietly measures how much support a household can offer. Kids lose time for sleep, sport, music, chores and plain boredom, which is where a surprising amount of curiosity begins.

Shorter does not mean softer. A teacher who assigns ten well-chosen problems will learn more about a class than one who assigns forty. Quality beats volume, and a tighter assignment is easier to mark and discuss the next day.

So set limits and keep them: roughly ten minutes per grade level, no weekend packets, and no penalty for stopping when the time is up. Let children come back to school rested and wanting more, rather than relieved to have survived the night.
