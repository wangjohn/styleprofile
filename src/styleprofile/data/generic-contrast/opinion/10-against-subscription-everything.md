# Against Subscription Everything

Last month I discovered that I was paying for a meditation app I had not opened since February. The charge was small, nine dollars, which is exactly why it had survived. Digging further, I found a cloud storage plan I did not remember choosing, a photo editor I used once, and a streaming service I had signed up for to watch a single series. The total came to over a hundred dollars a month, quietly leaving my account for things I barely touched.

I do not think I am unusual. Somewhere in the past decade, the way we buy things changed. Software, music, films, cars, razors, meal kits, fitness classes, even heated car seats have been converted from products you own into services you rent. Businesses love the arrangement, and it is easy to see why. But for the rest of us, subscription everything has become a bad bargain, and it is worth explaining why.

The core of the problem is a change in the basic relationship. When you buy a thing, you pay once and it is yours. You can use it, lend it, resell it, repair it, and keep it long after the manufacturer loses interest. When you subscribe, you pay indefinitely for access that the seller can alter or withdraw. The price may go up. Features you rely on may vanish or move behind a higher tier. The company may be acquired or shut down, and your library of purchases, which was never truly yours, disappears with it. Anyone who has watched a favorite film leave a streaming service, or lost access to documents when a plan lapsed, knows how fragile this arrangement is.

Consider the economics. Subscriptions are designed to be forgotten. Behavioral research on consumer habits suggests that people routinely underestimate the number of services they are paying for, and many companies depend on inertia to keep revenue flowing. Free trials roll over into paid plans. Cancellation, for some, requires a phone call during business hours, a series of screens asking you to reconsider, or a retention specialist with a discount offer. The Federal Trade Commission has taken action against firms for making cancellation harder than sign-up, and regulators in Europe have pushed for rules requiring that you be able to leave as easily as you joined. Yet the fact that such laws are necessary says something about the model. A product that people value does not need a labyrinth to keep them.

Over time, the sums can be punishing. A one-time purchase of a hundred and fifty dollars for software looks very different from a ten dollar monthly fee, which passes the same total in fifteen months and then continues forever. Photographers who once bought an editing program and used it for years now rent the same function at a recurring cost that never ends. Car manufacturers have experimented with charging monthly for features already installed in the vehicle, such as heated seats or remote start, effectively locking hardware you own behind a paywall. This is the logic carried to its absurd end: you buy the object, and still do not own what it can do.

Defenders make fair points. Subscriptions lower the barrier to entry, letting people try expensive tools for a short period without large upfront costs. For services that really do deliver continuing value, such as a news organization with reporters on the ground, a streaming library that keeps adding content, or software that is regularly updated against security threats, regular payment is reasonable. Developers argue, correctly, that maintaining software is a continuing expense, and that a one-time sale gives them little incentive to keep improving it. A steady income can support better, more stable products. I accept all of this. The issue is not that subscriptions should not exist. It is that they have spread to places where they add nothing but cost.

The clearest test is whether the seller keeps providing something new. A newspaper delivers every day, so a subscription fits. A word processor that mostly works the same way as it did five years ago, or a coffee mug that sends you a replacement each month for no reason you can explain, is trading on the habit of payment alone. When the continuing value is thin, the recurring fee is simply a way to extract more over time.

There is also a cost to our attention and our sense of control. Managing a dozen subscriptions takes work: tracking renewal dates, comparing tiers, watching for price hikes, remembering which email address each belongs to. Each one is a small cognitive burden, and together they weigh. Every service also wants to feel indispensable, so it sends notifications and nudges, competing for time you never agreed to give. A home full of owned objects is quieter than one full of accounts.

What should be done? Some changes can be made by individuals. Audit your bank statements every few months, and cancel what you do not use. Treat free trials with suspicion and set a calendar reminder the day you sign up. Where a one-time purchase is offered at a fair price, favor it, and tell the company why. Support software makers who sell perpetual licenses and open formats that let you take your files elsewhere. Rotate streaming services instead of keeping them all, subscribing for a month to watch what you want and leaving when you are done. Doing so will save money, and it sends a signal that loyalty cannot be assumed.

Other changes need rules. Regulators should require clear disclosure of recurring charges before payment, easy online cancellation, and reminders ahead of renewal, particularly after free trials. They should ban features that are already built into a product you have purchased from being held back for monthly fees. And they should consider protections for customers when a service ends, so that people are not left with nothing after years of payments. Consumer protection has always had to adapt to new business models, and this is the latest one that deserves attention.

Companies, for their part, could rediscover an old idea: earn the sale. Make something good, charge a fair price, and let customers decide when to return. Many of the best products ever made were sold this way, and the relationship was healthier for it. The seller had to keep making things worth buying, and the buyer was free to walk away without a fight.

I have since cancelled the meditation app, the extra storage and the photo editor. The savings are modest, but the feeling is out of proportion: a small recovery of control over my own money. I would like to live in a world where I own the things I pay for, and where the companies that want my money must ask for it again, in person, each time they have something worth buying.
