# Children Should Still Learn Cursive

Last spring I found a box of letters my grandmother wrote in the 1960s. The handwriting was a looping, slanted script that ran across the page like a river. I could read it easily, and I could also see her. There was a hurried stretch where the letters leaned forward, a tired evening where the lines drooped, and a joyful postscript squeezed into the margin. My own children, who were never taught cursive, could not read a word of it.

That small scene captures what we are about to lose. Over the past fifteen years, many school systems have dropped cursive from the curriculum, reasoning that keyboards have won and that classroom time is scarce. The reasoning sounds sensible. It is also wrong, and the schools that have quietly restored cursive instruction are right to do so.

Consider first the plain matter of literacy. A great deal of the written record is in cursive: family letters, diaries, historical documents, the marginal notes of teachers and doctors, the signatures on contracts. A person who cannot read handwriting is dependent on others to interpret their own heritage. Archivists already report that fewer young people can read original documents from even a century ago. We would not accept a school system that stopped teaching children to read old typefaces. Treating cursive as an optional extra is the same mistake in another form.

Then there is the question of how children learn. Research in cognitive science has repeatedly found that writing by hand engages the brain differently from typing. In studies of students taking notes, those who wrote longhand tended to process ideas more deeply, because they could not capture everything and had to decide what mattered. Brain imaging has shown richer activity in areas tied to memory and language when people write by hand. Cursive in particular asks for a continuous, controlled motion, linking letters into words and training fine motor skills that print and keyboards do not.

Teachers who work with struggling readers often say that cursive can help. Because the pencil rarely leaves the page, letters are harder to reverse, and words have a clearer shape. Some specialists in dyslexia recommend it for exactly this reason, though the evidence is mixed and I would not oversell it. What can be said with confidence is that handwriting practice supports spelling and composition, and that children who write fluently by hand tend to write more and with less strain.

The usual objection is that nobody writes by hand anymore. That is overstated. People still sign documents, fill in forms, jot down grocery lists, write birthday cards and take notes in meetings where a laptop would seem rude. Exams in many countries are still handwritten, and a student who writes slowly or illegibly is penalized for it. A fast, readable personal hand is a practical tool, and most adults who never learned one wish they had.

A second objection is that the curriculum is crowded. True, but cursive does not need much. A few weeks of focused instruction in the early grades, followed by regular practice in ordinary assignments, is enough for most children. That is a modest price for a skill that lasts a lifetime. Schools find time for far less useful things.

There is also a case that is harder to measure but no less real. Handwriting is personal. Each of us develops a hand that carries something of our temperament, and receiving a note in someone's own writing feels different from receiving a typed message in a standard font. Parents save their children's early scrawls for good reason. A signature is still treated as a mark of identity. In an age of automated text, a handwritten page is one of the few things that cannot be generated by pressing a button.

I do not want to turn this into nostalgia. No one should be forced to churn out copybook pages or be graded on the elegance of a capital Q. The aim is competence and comfort, not perfection. A child who learns to write a legible, connected script, and to read one, has gained something useful and lost nothing. Teaching cursive does not mean refusing keyboards; the two skills coexist easily, and children should learn typing too.

What troubles me is the way the decision was made. In many places cursive vanished not through a debate but through neglect, squeezed out by testing schedules and a vague sense that it was outdated. Nobody weighed the costs, because the costs are slow and diffuse. They show up years later, when a graduate cannot read a historical document, when a grandchild cannot decipher a recipe card, when a student discovers that taking notes by hand is, after all, how they think best.

Education should be conservative in one respect: it should hesitate before discarding what has served generations well. Cursive has earned a place in the classroom through utility, through its link to the written past, and through what it does for the developing mind. Let children learn it, and let them find, someday, a box of letters that they can read for themselves.
