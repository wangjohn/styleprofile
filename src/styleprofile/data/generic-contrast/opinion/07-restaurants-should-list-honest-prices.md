# Restaurants Should List Honest Prices, Fees Included

The burger on the menu says sixteen dollars. The bill, when it arrives, says twenty-three. Between those two numbers sit a three percent "kitchen appreciation" charge, a "wellness contribution" for staff health care, a service fee of eighteen percent that is not quite a tip, and sales tax calculated on top of all of it. Nobody lied, exactly. But nobody told the truth in the place where it counted.

This is the quiet scandal of modern dining, and it deserves a plain remedy: the price on the menu should be the price you pay, apart from tax and any tip you choose to give.

The practice of adding surcharges after the fact has spread across the industry because it works. A restaurant that lists a lower price looks cheaper than its competitors, and the customer discovers the difference only after eating. By then, leaving is impossible and complaining feels petty. Economists call this drip pricing, and decades of research show that it exploits a familiar weakness: once we have chosen something, we anchor to the first number we saw and accept later increases more readily than we should. The tactic is not an accident of bookkeeping. It is designed to take advantage of how people behave.

Defenders of these fees offer sympathetic reasons. Restaurants run on thin margins, wages and ingredient costs have risen, and owners want to cover health care or fairer pay without raising menu prices so much that customers stay home. Those pressures are real. I have no quarrel with a restaurant that decides to pay its cooks better. My quarrel is with the method. If the cost of running a business has gone up, the honest course is to put it in the price, where every diner can see it and compare.

Hiding costs in surcharges creates several harms at once. First, it makes comparison shopping impossible. A diner choosing between two restaurants cannot tell which one is truly cheaper when one adds twenty percent at the end and the other builds everything into its prices. The honest restaurant is punished for its honesty, appearing more expensive than a competitor that simply buried the difference. Second, it confuses the question of tipping. Many customers do not realize whether an automatic service charge goes to the servers, to the owner or to some blend of the two, and they end up tipping twice or not at all. Third, it erodes trust. People who feel tricked tend to spend less, return less often and tell their friends.

The law has begun to catch up. Several jurisdictions now require businesses to display an all-in price for tickets, hotel rooms and rental housing, and regulators have been looking at fees across other industries. California, for example, passed rules barring most businesses from advertising a price that excludes mandatory charges. Restaurants were initially treated more gently, and their carve-outs have been argued over ever since. Those exceptions should end. If a charge is mandatory, it is part of the price.

What would good practice look like? It is not complicated. A menu would show the full cost of each item, with no additional line for service, kitchen or wellness. If a restaurant wants to fund benefits, it raises the burger to nineteen dollars and says on the menu or website, if it wishes, that prices include a fair wage and health coverage. Tax would still be added, as it always is. Tipping would remain a voluntary decision. A restaurant that chooses to abolish tips can say so, and its prices would reflect it.

Some owners will worry that higher printed prices will scare customers away. In the short run, perhaps a few. But the evidence from restaurants that have switched to all-inclusive pricing is encouraging. Diners generally respond well to clarity, and the reputational gain can outweigh the first-glance sticker shock. Those that have made the move often report that servers earn more steadily and that guests complain less about the check. A transparent price is also a better foundation for the long-term relationship that every restaurant depends on.

There is a basic principle at stake. A price is a promise about what a purchase will cost. Every time a menu number turns out to be fiction, it teaches customers to distrust all numbers, and that makes ordinary commerce harder for the honest businesses as well as the sly ones. Markets function best when buyers can see what they are agreeing to before they agree.

So the next time you open a menu, you should be able to add up your order in your head and be right. That is a modest expectation, and one we hold for nearly everything else we buy. Restaurants that meet it will earn more goodwill than any appreciation fee could ever collect.
