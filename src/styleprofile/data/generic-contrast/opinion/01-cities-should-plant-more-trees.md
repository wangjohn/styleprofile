# Cities Should Plant More Trees

Walk down any city street on a July afternoon and you can feel the difference between a block with trees and a block without them. Under a canopy the air is several degrees cooler, the pavement is bearable, and people linger. On the bare block, heat radiates off asphalt and glass, and everyone hurries toward the nearest air conditioner.

That difference is not a matter of taste. Heat is the deadliest weather hazard most cities face, and the neighborhoods with the fewest trees are almost always the poorest ones. Planting is one of the few public investments that cools a city, cleans its air, soaks up stormwater and lifts mental health all at once. Studies have linked greener streets to lower rates of asthma, reduced stress and even fewer crimes. Few budget lines can claim so many returns.

The usual objections are real but small. Roots crack sidewalks, leaves clog gutters, and saplings die when nobody waters them. Those are problems of planning and maintenance, not reasons to stay paved. Cities can choose species suited to narrow strips, budget for the first three years of care, and pay crews to prune rather than cut down.

What is missing is ambition. Many cities set tree-cover targets and then miss them quietly. Councils should treat canopy as infrastructure, funded like water mains and measured every year, with the shadiest neighborhoods no longer getting the first plantings.

A tree costs little and works for a century. We should start digging.
