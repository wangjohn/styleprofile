Nobody sleeps the night before the last day of camp. That's just a law of nature, like gravity or mosquitoes.

I was twelve, and it was my third summer at Camp Whitefish, a scruffy place on a lake in northern Michigan with leaky cabins and a dining hall that smelled permanently of tater tots. By the end of July I'd stopped being homesick, which is its own kind of sad, because it meant I belonged somewhere that I'd have to leave. The counselors had been warning us all week. "Tomorrow's pack-up," they'd say in sing-song voices, like it was a party, and then we'd all go quiet.

We stayed up anyway, six of us in cabin four, whispering with flashlights under the blankets. Marcus, who had a gap in his front teeth and the loudest laugh I've ever heard, made us promise to write. We all promised. We all knew we wouldn't, not really, and that made the promising feel more serious, somehow.

Morning came gray and cool. Mist sat on the lake like a lid. We stripped the beds and stuffed sleeping bags into duffels that had been much bigger when we arrived, and I found a sock behind my bunk that I'm positive wasn't mine and kept it anyway.

Then there was the thing we did every year, the last-day ritual. Everyone gathers at the dock before breakfast. You're supposed to bring one object from the summer, anything, and drop it into the lake while saying a word. Just one word. The idea, according to the director, a gentle man named Mr. Halvorsen with a beard like a squirrel's nest, was that the lake keeps what you give it.

I'd thought about my object all night. I had a friendship bracelet, a blue and orange thing I'd made badly in arts and crafts, but I couldn't part with it. I had a bottle cap from a root beer I'd shared with Marcus. In the end I grabbed a smooth gray stone from the shore that morning, nothing special, and held it in my fist.

When it was my turn, I stood at the edge of the dock and the boards creaked under my sneakers, and I realized I couldn't think of a single word. Not one. My mind was a blank, white and loud. Behind me, I could hear younger kids shifting, someone sniffling.

"Thanks," I said. That was it. And I let go.

The stone went in with barely a sound, a little plunk, a ring of ripples that spread and faded. I stood there longer than I needed to. Marcus bumped my shoulder with his, and when I looked over he had tears all down his face and was grinning like an idiot.

"Thanks," he said, quieter, and threw a pinecone.

Breakfast was strange. Everyone was loud in a brittle way, shoving, laughing at nothing, trading addresses on napkins and the backs of hands. A girl named Beth from the next cabin over cried openly into her pancakes, and nobody teased her, which tells you everything about what kind of day it was. Even the cook came out of the kitchen, wiping his hands, and shook a few hundred small palms.

At ten the buses arrived, yellow and impatient. Parents began trickling in with sunglasses and cameras, calling names across the lawn. I saw my mom wave from the gravel lot and my chest did something complicated. I was so glad to see her, and I was furious that she'd come.

I hugged my counselor, a lanky college kid named Dev who had taught me to paddle a canoe straight, and he ruffled my hair and said, "You were a good one, kid." I carried that sentence around for years. I think I still do.

Marcus and I did the handshake we'd invented, the one with the snap and the elbow, three times over, getting it wrong on purpose. Then his dad called him, and he went, and he turned around twice to wave.

In the car, my mom asked me a hundred questions, bless her. Was the food okay? Did I make friends? Did I remember to use sunscreen? I answered in single words, looking out the window at the pines going by, the road curling away from the lake, and somewhere in there I stopped being able to see Camp Whitefish at all.

"You okay, bug?" she asked.

"Yeah," I said. "I think I just left something there."

She didn't ask what. She reached over and squeezed my knee, and we drove on, and the radio played some song about the summer ending, which felt a little rude of it, honestly.

Marcus did write, by the way. Once. A postcard, a picture of a moose. It said, in terrible handwriting, "THANKS." I still have it.
