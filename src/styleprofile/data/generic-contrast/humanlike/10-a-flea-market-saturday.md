I left the house at six-fifteen with a thermos of coffee, forty dollars in small bills, and a firm promise to myself that I would not come home with another lamp.

I came home with two lamps. Let's get that out of the way.

The flea market sets up in a gravel lot behind an old bowling alley, every Saturday from April until the weather turns, and I've been going for about four years. It's not fancy. There are no artisanal anything, no little signs about provenance. Just rows of folding tables and open truck beds and blankets spread on the ground, and on them, everything. Chipped teacups. Hand tools with wooden handles worn smooth by someone else's palms. Boxes of postcards that say things like "Having a wonderful time, weather fine, Gerald snores." Stacks of vinyl, a crate of mismatched doorknobs, a taxidermied squirrel dressed as a tiny pirate.

I don't actually need any of it. That's sort of the point.

Early is the key. The serious people show up before the sun, with flashlights strapped to their foreheads, and they move through the stalls with the focused calm of surgeons. I'm not one of them. I'm more of a wanderer, a sipper of coffee, a person who lingers. But I like the early light, low and gold, catching on glass and chrome, turning a table of old bottles into something almost holy.

The first stall I always visit belongs to a man named Hollis, who sells radios. Old ones, tube radios and bakelite things and a couple of enormous console models he somehow hauls here in the back of a pickup. He's in his seventies and wears the same green cap every week, and he'll talk to you about any of them for as long as you'll stand there. This morning I asked about a little red one on the corner of his table, and he spent a full ten minutes telling me about its manufacturer, its previous owner (a dentist in Dayton), and the particular tone it makes when it warms up.

"Turn it on," he said. "Listen."

It crackled, then hummed, then a faint voice drifted out, some local station playing a song from before I was born. I stood there in the gravel with my coffee going cold and felt oddly emotional about it. Hollis watched my face and just nodded, like he'd seen it before.

I didn't buy the radio. I want to be clear that I have limits. But I thanked him, and he gave me a butterscotch candy from his pocket, and that was a fine trade.

Around eight, the crowd thickens. Families with strollers, couples in matching sneakers, college kids hunting for ironic sweaters. The smell changes too. Fried dough from the food truck at the end, onions on a grill, the sweet dusty smell of old paper. I wandered through a stretch of book tables and picked up a cookbook from 1962 that includes a recipe for something called "Salad Surprise" involving lime gelatin and shredded carrots. The surprise, I think, is that anyone ate it. A dollar. Obviously mine.

There's an art to haggling, and I'm not good at it. I tend to either pay the sticker price out of politeness or ask for a discount in a voice so apologetic that the seller feels bad and gives me one anyway. My friend Marguerite, who comes with me sometimes, is a master. She'll pick up a thing, turn it over, sigh, and say, "Oh, it's lovely, but..." and then trail off with the saddest face. Nine times out of ten the price drops by a third. She calls it "the trailing off." I've tried it and I just look like I forgot what I was saying.

Today she wasn't there, so I was on my own, and I did all right. I bargained a woman down from eight dollars to six for a set of four brass drawer pulls shaped like tiny acorns. I don't own a dresser that needs them. I'll find one.

At nine-thirty I hit the stall I'd been dreading, the one with the lamps.

It's run by a pair of brothers, never introduced, who sell electrical odds and ends and who always have at least one lamp that looks like it was made for a villain's study. This week it was a tall brass thing with a green glass shade, the kind you imagine in a library full of leather chairs. Forty-five dollars, which was more than I had left. I stood there, wavering, doing the math, trying to look casual.

"It works," one of the brothers said, flicking the switch. A warm light bloomed up. "Wiring's new."

"I really don't need a lamp."

"Nobody needs a lamp," he said. "You need a light that makes you feel something."

That was a pretty good line, and I think he knew it. I bought the lamp. I talked him down to thirty-eight, helped by the fact that I had to put back the acorns, and then I sheepishly bought the acorns again with the leftover bills. Fine. That's how it goes.

The second lamp was a small blue ceramic one, shaped like a swan, and it was two dollars, so it barely counts.

By eleven I was tired and sun-warmed and carrying more than I could comfortably manage, a bag in each hand and the green lamp tucked under my arm like a very awkward child. I stopped for a cup of lemonade from a girl of about ten, running a stand beside her grandmother's table of quilts. She charged me a dollar fifty and gave me a speech about the lemons, which she said were "organic, I think." I tipped her a dollar and she accepted it with the gravity of a banker.

Her grandmother, a small woman with silver braids, asked what I'd found. I showed her the acorns and she approved. Then she pointed at the lamp and made a noise, a kind of chuckle.

"You're one of those," she said.

"One of what?"

"You buy things because they're looking at you."

I laughed, because it's true. That's exactly it. I'm not a collector, not really, and I'm not a bargain hunter. I just feel a pull toward things that have outlived their first owners and still have a bit of dignity. There's a quiet in an old object. Someone used this. Someone wound that clock every Sunday, or kept this mug on their desk through twenty winters. Their fingers wore the finish off the handle. And now it's sitting in a lot in the sun, patient, waiting for a person to say, I'll take you.

I'm aware this is a ridiculous way to think about a gravel lot behind a bowling alley. I don't mind.

On my way out I passed Hollis again and he was packing up, wrapping the little red radio in a towel, and he lifted a hand in farewell. I lifted the lamp, awkwardly, and he laughed so hard he had to steady himself on the truck bed.

At home I put everything on the kitchen table and looked at the haul. A lamp with a villain's glow. A ceramic swan. A cookbook with a lime gelatin crime in it. Four brass acorns. I plugged in the lamp and the room changed, got deeper and slower, and I sat there in the new light feeling pleasantly foolish.

Is it a good use of forty-odd dollars and a Saturday morning? I honestly don't know how you'd measure that. I'd say the lemonade alone was worth it, and the butterscotch, and the crackle of a stranger's radio from a dentist's old waiting room in Dayton.

Next week, I'm bringing a shopping list. I'm going to write on it, in large letters, NO LAMPS. And then I'll go and stand in front of the brothers' table, and I'll look at whatever new thing is glowing there, and I already know how this story ends.
