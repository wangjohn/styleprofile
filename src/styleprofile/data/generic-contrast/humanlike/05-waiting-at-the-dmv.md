Number 4172. That was me. The screen above the counter said they were serving 4118.

I did the math right there in my plastic chair, which is a thing I do when I'm anxious, and it didn't help. Fifty-four people ahead of me, three windows open out of eight, one of which closed as I watched because the woman working it stood up, put on her coat, and walked away without a word. Nobody else reacted. Everyone in that room had clearly made peace with the place a long time ago, and I was the only fresh-faced fool still hoping for efficiency.

I'd brought a book. I'd also brought snacks, because a friend warned me, and I'm glad, because there's no vending machine. There is a poster about eye exams. I read it four times.

Around the second hour I started noticing the little community that forms. The man to my left, in a work vest, had been there since the doors opened, and he gave me the kind of nod you'd give a fellow soldier. A teenager across the aisle was getting a learner's permit and bouncing her knee so hard her whole row rattled. Her mother kept whispering "stop it" and then, a minute later, doing the same thing with her own foot. I wanted to hug them both.

A toddler found a pen somewhere and drew carefully on the armrest of a chair, and not one adult stopped him. That felt right, somehow. Like he was the only one of us acting sensibly.

When my number finally flashed, I almost didn't hear it. I'd drifted off into a daydream about sandwiches. The clerk at window six was a calm, tired man named Walter (his tag said so), and he took my forms, squinted at them, and said, "Oh, you need the other form."

I stared at him.

"Kidding," he said. "You're good." And he smiled, a real one, and I laughed out loud, a little too hard, and the people in the nearby chairs looked at me like I'd cracked.

The whole transaction took six minutes. Six. After three hours and ten minutes of waiting, the actual business of being a licensed adult was over before I could finish my thank-you.

Walking out, I passed the next batch of arrivals taking their tickets with that bright, doomed look I'd worn that morning. I wanted to warn them, tell them to bring snacks and to expect the eye exam poster. But you can't really explain it. You have to sit in the chair yourself.
