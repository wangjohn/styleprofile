Nobody warns you that the hardest part of cooking for one is the leftovers.

I mean that literally. A recipe that serves four is a recipe that serves me for four days, and by Thursday I'm eating the same chili with a growing sense of betrayal. Recipes are written for families, for dinner parties, for people with a table full of faces. They are not written for a guy standing alone in his kitchen at seven on a Wednesday, holding half an onion and wondering what to do with the other half.

I started cooking for myself about a year and a half ago, after a breakup, which is such a cliché that I barely want to mention it. But it's true. For six years, cooking had been a shared project. She chopped, I stirred, we argued gently about salt. When she left, she took the good knife (fair, it was hers) and a certain atmosphere, and I was left with a kitchen that felt too big and too quiet.

For the first month I basically didn't cook. Cereal. Toast. A rotating cast of frozen burritos that I ate standing at the counter, like a raccoon. And I want to be honest, there's nothing wrong with any of that, but around week five I caught myself eating a burrito in the dark and thought, this is a bit sad, isn't it?

So I decided to try. Not to be impressive. Just to be a person who sits down for dinner.

The early attempts were bad. I made a pot of rice that turned into a kind of glue. I burned garlic so thoroughly that the smoke alarm sang for three minutes while I fanned it with a dish towel and apologized to nobody. I roasted a chicken thigh that came out gray, undersalted, and oddly rubbery, and ate the whole thing anyway out of stubbornness.

What helped, strangely, was lowering the stakes. I stopped trying to make meals and started making small things. A fried egg on toast, done well, with the yolk still loose. A bowl of pasta with butter, black pepper and a lot of parmesan, three ingredients and ten minutes. Roasted carrots with a little honey, because I discovered that an oven on its own, left alone, does most of the work for you.

I got a small cast iron pan at a yard sale for six dollars. It's the best thing I own. It holds heat like a grudge, and when I sear a piece of salmon in it, skin down, listening to that hard crackle, I feel for a moment like I know what I'm doing.

There are tricks to cooking for one that I've learned the hard way. Buy the smaller things. Half a cabbage, if the store will sell it, or a couple of tomatoes instead of a bag. Freeze what you can: soup in single servings, bread in slices, ginger in a knobby little lump you can grate straight from the freezer. Cook a base on Sunday (a pot of beans, say, or a tray of roasted vegetables) and rearrange it through the week so it doesn't feel like the same dinner twice. Tuesday, those beans go over rice. Wednesday, they're in a quesadilla. Thursday, I blend them into a soup with whatever's wilting in the fridge.

I also learned that eating alone doesn't have to be eating badly. This one took me a while. For years I'd had this idea that a real meal required an audience, that making something nice just for yourself was a kind of indulgence you hadn't earned. But the truth is the opposite. I'm the only person who has to live in my body every day. I might as well feed it well.

So now I set the table. Not with candles, I'm not that far gone. But a real plate, a fork on the left, a glass of water. I put the phone in the other room. I eat slowly and I taste things, the sharp edge of lemon, the way thyme smells a bit like a summer afternoon. Some nights I put on a record. Some nights it's the radio, a man talking about the weather in a voice like warm gravel.

There's a particular joy in the small private victories. Last month I made a risotto, a proper one, standing at the stove for forty minutes, ladling in broth one scoop at a time and stirring until my wrist ached. It came out creamy and loose and a little bit magical. I ate it at the table and wanted to tell someone. I actually said "wow" out loud, to the empty room, and then laughed at myself.

I did call my mother afterward, to tell her about it. She was thrilled and then immediately told me I was using too much butter. Which, fair.

The loneliness hasn't vanished, to be clear. Some nights it comes in with the dusk, and I look at the second chair across from me and feel it. Cooking doesn't fix that. I don't think anything does, exactly. But it gives the evening a shape. It gives my hands something to do while my head catches up. There's a rhythm to it, the chopping, the heat, the smell rising, and by the time I sit down I'm usually in a better mood than when I started.

And sometimes I invite people over, which is a risk I've learned to take. A friend named Joanna came last month, and I made her the pasta I make for myself, nothing fancy, and she said it was the best thing she'd eaten all week. I didn't believe her, but I loved that she said it. We ate at the little table by the window and talked until the candle (okay, I do own a candle) burned down.

After she left, I did the dishes slowly, humming. The kitchen didn't feel too big anymore. It just felt like mine.

I still hate the leftover problem, for the record. Yesterday I looked into the fridge and found three containers of mystery soup, each in a different shade of beige, and I stood there trying to remember what any of them was. I picked one by a coin toss. It turned out to be lentil. Honestly, not bad.

Tonight it's an egg on toast again. I might add some chili crisp, if I'm feeling wild. It takes about six minutes, and I'll sit at the table and eat it properly, and that will be dinner, and that will be fine. More than fine, actually. It's the kind of small ordinary good thing I would have sneered at once, and I'd like to apologize to my younger self about that, if he's listening.
