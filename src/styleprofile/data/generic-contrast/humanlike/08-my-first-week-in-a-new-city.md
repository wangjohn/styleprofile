Day one, I got lost walking to the grocery store, and the grocery store was across the street.

That's not an exaggeration. I'd moved to Portland in late September for a job I was still half convinced I'd gotten by mistake, and I stood on the sidewalk outside my new apartment with a canvas bag and a phone showing a little blue dot that kept spinning in circles. The store was right there. I could see the sign. But the blue dot said I was in a parking lot two blocks north, and I trusted the dot over my own eyes, and ended up walking a loop around a church.

I eventually got the groceries. I bought a loaf of bread, a bag of oranges, and a jar of peanut butter, because those felt like the foods of a person who intended to stay. Then I went home to my apartment, which was mostly boxes, and sat on the floor eating a sandwich off a paper towel and thinking, well, here we are.

The thing nobody tells you about a new city is how many small decisions it demands. Which grocery store. Which coffee place. Which side of the street the bus stops on, and whether it matters, and which bus. At home I'd had a hundred defaults I never thought about. Here every single morning was a tiny exam.

Tuesday was my first day at work. My new coworkers were kind in a slightly overwhelming way, the way people are when they're trying hard to make you feel included. A woman named Ingrid gave me a tour, pointing at doors and saying names I immediately forgot. A guy named Calvin showed me the kitchen, where there was a labeled shelf for everyone's oat milk, and I stood there feeling very far from anything I understood. I ate lunch alone at my desk, a little ashamed of the pb and orange situation.

By Wednesday I was exhausted in a way I couldn't explain. I hadn't done anything strenuous. But my brain had been running on high alert for three days, memorizing street names, rehearsing small talk, translating the tone of every email. When I got home that night I lay on the couch, which was the only furniture I'd assembled, and stared at the ceiling for an hour without a thought in my head.

Thursday I did something slightly brave. I walked to a coffee shop I'd spotted on my commute, a narrow little place with mismatched chairs and a chalkboard menu in handwriting so loopy I couldn't read half of it. I ordered something that sounded like a lavender latte and wasn't, quite, and sat by the window. And I just watched people for a while. A man walking a very old, very slow greyhound. Two teenagers sharing earbuds. A woman in a bright yellow raincoat, riding a bike with a cello strapped to her back, who looked at me through the glass and, I swear, nodded.

It's funny what gets to you. I'd been fine, more or less, about the big stuff, the boxes and the new job and the distance from my friends. But that nod made my throat go tight. Somebody had acknowledged me. I existed here now.

Friday was the day I nearly gave up.

It rained, hard, all morning, and my umbrella (a different story, an old one) was somewhere in a box labeled MISC. I got to work soaked to the knees. The heat in my building was broken. A meeting ran long and I said something in it that came out wrong, a joke that landed with the weight of a dropped plate, and the room went silent for one terrible second before somebody coughed and moved on.

I went home that evening feeling like I'd made a huge mistake. I called my sister, and she let me complain for ten minutes without interrupting, which is her great talent. Then she said, "Did you eat?" and I said, "Toast," and she said, "Go get actual food. Go out. Sit somewhere with people."

I didn't want to. I wanted to stay in my damp socks and feel sorry for myself. But I put on a jacket and went out, mostly so I could tell her I had, and wandered a few blocks until I found a place with a warm yellow window and a line of people at the counter. A noodle shop. Eight stools, a cook who shouted orders, steam on the glass.

I sat at the counter between a woman reading a paperback and a man who was clearly a regular, because the cook slid him a bowl without asking. I ordered what the regular was having, pointing, since I couldn't pronounce it. When it came, it was enormous and fragrant and almost too hot to eat, and I bent over it and breathed it in, and something in my chest let go.

"First time?" the man asked, not looking up.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You're holding your chopsticks like a hammer," he said, and grinned, and showed me how to do it properly. His name was Desmond. He'd lived in the neighborhood twenty years and had opinions about every restaurant within a mile, which he shared with me generously, along with a ranking of the best bakeries and a warning about a certain stretch of road that floods.

We talked for an hour. I walked home with a list on a napkin and a stomach full of broth, and the rain had stopped, and the streets were shining under the lamps. I got lost again, a little, on the way back. But somehow it didn't bother me. I just took a different turn, and then another one, and found my building anyway.

Saturday I slept until nine, an act of rebellion. Then I unpacked, finally, with the windows open and music on, which felt like a small ceremony. I hung a map on the wall. I put the cheap succulent on the sill, the one Ingrid had given me, which I'd been too embarrassed to bring home on Tuesday. I found my umbrella, naturally, in the very last box.

In the afternoon I went back to the noodle shop. Desmond wasn't there, but the cook recognized me, I think, and gave me a nod of his own, and I took the same stool and ordered the same thing and didn't need to point.

On the walk back, I caught myself taking a shortcut without checking my phone. A cut-through between two buildings, a wooden fence with a cat sitting on it, a little alley that smelled of rosemary. I stopped at the end of it, because I realized what I'd done. I knew where I was going. Seven days, and a piece of this place had settled into my feet.

Sunday I did nothing, wonderfully. I read, I made real dinner (rice, eggs, whatever greens I could find), I washed the dishes right away like a responsible adult. At some point I opened the window and listened to the street, the buses sighing at the curb, somebody laughing three floors down, a dog barking at nothing. It wasn't home. Not yet. But it was starting to sound like a place a person could live.

I won't pretend the week was easy, or that I've figured it all out. I haven't. I still trust that blue dot too much. I still say the wrong thing in meetings, sometimes. But I think about that yellow raincoat and the cello, the nod through the window, and I figure a city is mostly just a lot of small kindnesses stacked on top of each other until one day you notice you're standing on them.

I bought another loaf of bread on the way home. I'm planning to stay.
