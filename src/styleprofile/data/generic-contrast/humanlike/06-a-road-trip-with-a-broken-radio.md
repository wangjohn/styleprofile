The radio died somewhere outside Amarillo, and for a good twenty minutes neither of us admitted it.

My brother Tom was driving, because it was his car, and I was in the passenger seat with my feet on the dashboard, which he hates. We'd been going for two days. The plan was simple: Chicago to Albuquerque, nine hundred and some miles, to help our aunt move into a smaller house. We'd made a playlist, argued about the playlist, and then abandoned the playlist for whatever the stations gave us. And then, mid-chorus of a song I didn't even like, the sound just thinned out into a hiss and stopped.

Tom fiddled with the knob. I fiddled with the knob. We both pressed the little buttons like they'd forgive us if we were gentle enough. Nothing. The dash lit up politely, the screen said the right station name, and the speakers produced the exact amount of music you'd get from a potato.

"Okay," Tom said. "So we talk."

I laughed. We are not people who talk, not for long. We're the kind of siblings who text each other memes during holidays so we don't have to make eye contact over the turkey. But there we were, with six hundred miles left and nothing else to do.

So we started with the easy stuff. Work, mostly. His job in logistics, which he explained to me for ten minutes and I still can't tell you what he does. Mine, which is simpler and sadder. Then somewhere around the Texas panhandle, the flat gold land stretching out in every direction like the world had been ironed, we hit a stretch of silence, and it wasn't uncomfortable. That surprised me.

Then Tom said, out of nowhere, "Do you remember the car sickness thing?"

I did. When we were kids, our dad drove us to the lake every August, and I'd get sick about forty minutes in, every single year, without fail. Dad would pull over and hold my hair back and say the same joke, something about how I was feeding the ditch. Tom had been maybe nine. He'd stand by the car with his arms crossed, annoyed that we were late, and then, quietly, he'd hand me a juice box from the cooler.

"I did that on purpose," he said now. "The juice. Because you looked so miserable."

I had always assumed it was an accident. A kid grabbing a drink for himself and finding me there instead. Thirty years, and I'd never given him credit.

We stopped for gas in a town whose name I never caught. A woman at the counter sold us a bag of peanuts and a gas station sandwich that had seen things, and she looked at our faces and asked if we were okay. We said sure. We were great, actually. We got back in the car and she waved.

The rest of that day, we sang. Badly. Tom has a surprisingly decent baritone, which I didn't know, and he did a whole bit where he'd lead with an old song's first line and I had to guess the next. We got a lot wrong. I think I invented about half the lyrics to a song by a band that I'm now fairly sure doesn't exist.

At dusk, the sky went a color I don't have a good word for. Orange, then pinkish, then something almost green near the edge. We pulled over on a dirt shoulder and stood outside the car for a while, not saying much, just watching the horizon go out like a lamp.

"We should do this more often," Tom said.

"Drive across the country with a broken radio?"

"Sure. Why not."

I told him it'd cost a fortune in gas, and he said that's what the sandwiches were for, and I couldn't argue.

Our aunt's house was small and warm and smelled like lemon cleaner when we finally got there, a day and a half later than we'd planned because Tom insisted on stopping at a roadside dinosaur. We unloaded boxes for hours. At some point my aunt asked how the drive had been, and Tom and I looked at each other, and neither of us had an answer that would fit in a sentence.

"Quiet," I said finally.

"Good quiet," Tom added.

On the way home, a mechanic in Joliet fixed the radio in about four minutes. A loose wire, he said, shrugging, like the whole thing had been nothing. Tom paid him and we sat in the lot with the engine running while music came blasting back, loud and bright and a little too eager.

We turned it off. Not for good, just for the first hour. Old habits, I guess, though they were new ones, really.
