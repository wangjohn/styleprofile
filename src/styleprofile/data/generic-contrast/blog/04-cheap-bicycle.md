# What a Cheap Bicycle Taught Me About Maintenance

I bought my first adult bicycle for ninety dollars from a classified listing. It was a heavy steel commuter with fading green paint, a squeaky chain, and a seat that tilted slightly to the left. The seller shrugged when I asked about its history. I rode it home grinning, certain I had found a bargain.

For the first month, it was a bargain. Then the problems began, one at a time. The brakes went soft. The rear tire kept going flat. The chain started skipping under load. Each issue arrived at a bad moment, usually when I was already late, and each time I treated it as an emergency rather than a predictable event. I would wheel the bike to a shop, pay a hurried fee, and promise myself I would take better care of it.

I never did, until a flat tire on a rainy Tuesday left me walking two miles in dress shoes. That evening I watched a few videos, bought a basic tool kit and a patch set, and spent a Saturday morning learning what my bike actually consisted of. It turned out to be simple. A chain, two wheels, a few cables, some bolts. Nothing mysterious.

The lessons were small but lasting. First, cheap things are not necessarily bad, but they demand more attention. A well-made bicycle forgives neglect for longer. A cheap one tells you immediately when you have ignored it. In a way that is useful, because the feedback is fast and honest.

Second, maintenance is mostly about frequency, not skill. Wiping and oiling the chain every couple of weeks takes five minutes and prevents most of the drama. Checking tire pressure before a ride takes thirty seconds. These tiny habits cost almost nothing compared to the repairs they avoid.

Third, understanding a machine changes how you feel about it. Once I knew how my brakes worked, a squeal was no longer a source of dread; it was information. I could adjust a cable, tighten a bolt, and keep going.

I still ride that green bicycle. It has new tires, better brake pads, and a seat that finally sits level. Altogether I have spent about twice the original price on it, and it has repaid me in commutes, errands, and weekend wandering. More than that, it taught me that attention is the cheapest form of upkeep, and that most things last far longer when someone simply notices them regularly.
