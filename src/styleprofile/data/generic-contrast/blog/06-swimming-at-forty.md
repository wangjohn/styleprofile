# Learning to Swim at Forty

I grew up near water and never learned to swim. That sounds contradictory, but it is true. There was a lake a ten-minute walk from my childhood home, and every summer my friends splashed around in it while I stood on the dock with a towel, claiming I had a headache or simply preferred to watch. I was afraid, and I was embarrassed about being afraid, and the embarrassment kept the fear alive for thirty years.

At forty, I decided I was finished with it. Nothing dramatic happened to prompt this. I was at a friend's wedding on a lakeside property, and while everyone else jumped off a floating platform for photographs, I stayed on the shore holding people's shoes. Somewhere in the middle of that afternoon I felt, quite clearly, that I was tired of it. Tired of the excuses, the careful positioning near the shallow end, and the small private shame.

The next week I signed up for adult lessons at a community pool. I will be honest: walking into that building was one of the harder things I have done. The air smelled of chlorine and the echo of splashing made my chest tighten. My class turned out to be six people, ranging from their twenties to their sixties, all of us standing in waist-deep water wearing the same nervous expression. That shared nervousness helped enormously. Nobody was performing for anyone.

Our instructor, a patient woman named Dana, started with things that felt almost insultingly simple. Put your face in the water. Blow bubbles. Open your eyes underwater. For the first two lessons, that was essentially all we did. I remember thinking that I was wasting my money. Then I realized that I had never once put my whole face in water voluntarily, and that this small act was the actual barrier. Everything else was technique.

The turning point came in the fourth week, when Dana had us float on our backs while she held a hand under each of our shoulders. She gradually let go, and I did not notice until she stepped away. I was floating. My ears were underwater, the ceiling lights were shimmering above me, and for a few seconds I felt completely supported by something I had feared my whole life. I started laughing and promptly swallowed a mouthful of water, which ended the moment, but I never forgot it.

Progress after that was uneven. Some weeks I felt like a natural; others I sank like a stone and wondered why I had bothered. Learning as an adult is different from learning as a child. Children do not seem to mind failing in public. Adults carry decades of self-image, and every flailing stroke feels like evidence against it. I had to keep reminding myself that being a beginner was the entire point.

Breathing was my biggest struggle. In freestyle you turn your head to the side to gasp air in rhythm with your stroke, and my body refused to coordinate. I would swim two lengths, run out of breath, and cling to the wall panting. Dana suggested I slow down dramatically, and that advice, counterintuitive as it sounded, fixed nearly everything. Fear had been making me rush, and rushing made me breathless.

Six months in, I swam a full length of the pool without stopping. It was not graceful. A teenager in the next lane passed me twice. But I reached the far wall, held on, and felt a surge of pride out of all proportion to the distance.

This summer I went back to that lake. I walked off the end of the dock and dropped into the water, cold and green and surprisingly gentle. My friends cheered from the shore, which was a little embarrassing and also wonderful. I swam out about thirty yards, rolled onto my back, and floated there for a while, looking at the sky.

What I took from this is not really about swimming. It is that fear can be remarkably specific, and that it often shrinks when you approach it in tiny steps with other people beside you. I wasted a lot of summers on that dock. I do not regret the lessons, only the waiting.
