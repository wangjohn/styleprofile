# A Year of Learning Spanish in the Evenings

Last September I decided to learn Spanish. I had no trip planned and no relatives to impress. I simply realized one night that I had been telling myself I would learn another language since college, and that two decades of saying so had produced nothing. I downloaded an app, set a reminder for eight-thirty each evening, and began. A year later, I can report some modest results and several surprises.

Let me start with what I can actually do now, so that expectations are calibrated. I can order a meal, ask for directions, and chat about the weather, my job, and my weekend plans. I can read a children's book without much help and a newspaper article with a dictionary open. I can follow a slow conversation, though fast native speech still washes over me like a rushing stream. I am not fluent, and I would not claim to be. But I am far beyond the person who started, and that progress feels real.

The first lesson was about consistency. I committed to thirty minutes every evening, and for the first two months I barely missed a day. Then I got sick, fell out of the routine for a week, and nearly quit. What saved me was lowering the bar. On bad days, I allowed myself to do just five minutes: one lesson, a handful of flashcards, anything. Five minutes kept the streak alive and reminded me that the habit mattered more than any single session. Most nights, once I sat down, I ended up doing the full half hour anyway.

Second, I learned that apps are a good start and a poor finish. The app I used was excellent for vocabulary and basic grammar, and I would recommend it to any beginner. But around month four I noticed that I could translate sentences on the screen and yet freeze completely when someone spoke to me. Recognition and production are different skills. So I added a weekly conversation session with a tutor online, and that changed everything. Speaking forced me to retrieve words under pressure, to make mistakes in real time, and to survive them. My tutor, a patient woman from Colombia, corrected me gently and never made me feel foolish, though I certainly made plenty of errors.

Third, the grammar was both better and worse than I expected. Verb conjugation looked terrifying at first, with its endless tables of forms. Over time, patterns emerged, and what looked like chaos turned out to follow rules, with a handful of stubborn exceptions. The subjunctive mood, which I had dreaded, was less of a monster than its reputation, though I still use it clumsily. What I did not anticipate was how much the little words would trouble me. The difference between ser and estar, two verbs that both mean "to be," took me months to feel instead of merely memorize. I still sometimes hesitate before choosing.

Fourth, I discovered that content I enjoy accelerates learning more than any exercise. Around the six-month mark I began watching Spanish-language shows with subtitles, first in English and later in Spanish. I picked a detective series, which turned out to be an excellent choice because the plots were gripping enough to keep me watching even when I understood only half the dialogue. I also found a few podcasts for learners, listening during dishes and walks. Exposure of this kind gave me a sense of the language's rhythm and its everyday phrases, things no textbook quite captures.

There were frustrations, and I want to be honest about them. Progress is not linear. Some weeks I felt I was improving daily; others I seemed to forget everything I knew. There were plateaus that lasted a month or more, during which I wondered if the effort was worth it. As an adult learner, I also had to confront an unwelcome truth: my brain is simply slower at absorbing language than a child's. I needed to repeat words many more times than I expected before they stuck. Accepting that without self-criticism was a skill of its own.

I also felt self-conscious. Speaking in a second language strips away the sophistication you normally project. In English I can be witty and precise; in Spanish I sound like a polite toddler. At first that stung my pride. Eventually I came to see it as humbling in a useful way. It gave me new empathy for anyone who has ever spoken to me in a language that was not their first, and I now listen to those people with more patience.

A few practical habits made the biggest difference, and I will list them in case they help someone. I kept a small notebook of new words and reviewed it on the train. I labeled a few household objects with sticky notes, which sounds childish but works. I wrote three or four sentences in a journal each weekend, then had my tutor correct them. And I tried to think in Spanish for small moments, narrating my own actions: "Voy a preparar café." Silly, perhaps, but those tiny exercises built a bridge between study and ordinary life.

The unexpected reward has been the way the language opened up my surroundings. I live in a city with a large Spanish-speaking community, and I had walked past signs, menus, and conversations for years without understanding them. Now I catch fragments. I can chat with the woman at my local bakery, who seemed delighted when I first tried, and I overheard a neighbor singing a song I recognized from one of my shows. These moments feel like small doors opening in a wall I had not realized was there.

What comes next? I plan to keep going. My goal for the coming year is to hold a twenty-minute conversation without switching to English, and to read an actual novel in Spanish. I also hope to travel somewhere the language is spoken and try it out in the wild, where mistakes carry real consequences and real rewards. Maybe next autumn I will write a post about that trip.

If you have been putting off a language, I would say this: the evenings will pass regardless. A year goes by whether you fill it or not. Thirty minutes a day does not sound like much, but it adds up to more than a hundred and eighty hours, and that is enough to change what you can do. Start badly, start small, and keep showing up. The rest tends to follow.
