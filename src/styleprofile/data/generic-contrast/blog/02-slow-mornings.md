# The Case for Slow Mornings

Most mornings used to feel like a race I had already lost. The alarm went off, I grabbed my phone, and within minutes I was reading messages, answering questions, and gulping coffee while hunting for my keys. By the time I got to work I was tired and a little irritable, and the day had barely started.

A few months ago I tried something simple. I set my alarm thirty minutes earlier and made one rule: no screens until I had eaten breakfast. That was all. I made tea, sat by the window, and did nothing in particular. Sometimes I read a few pages of a novel. Sometimes I just watched the neighbor's cat patrol the fence.

The change surprised me. Those quiet minutes gave my mind room to wake up on its own schedule, instead of being yanked into other people's priorities. I noticed I was less reactive during the day. Small annoyances, like a delayed train or a clumsy email, bothered me less, as if I had stored up a little patience before leaving the house.

A slow morning is not about productivity, though it does seem to help. It is about starting the day as yourself rather than as a responder. You do not need an elaborate routine or an hour of meditation. You need a bit of time, a warm drink, and permission to be unhurried.

If your mornings feel frantic, try borrowing thirty minutes from sleep or from the evening. See what happens.
