# A Note on Thank-You Cards

My grandmother wrote thank-you notes for everything. A casserole, a ride to the airport, a birthday phone call. As a kid I found this faintly ridiculous. Why mail a card to someone you would see on Sunday anyway? It took me decades, and a few awkward silences of my own, to understand what she was doing.

A thank-you card is a small piece of evidence that someone's kindness landed. Texts are fine and quick, and I send plenty of them, but they vanish into a scroll of other messages. A card sits on a shelf or a refrigerator. People keep them. I have a friend who admitted she still has a note I wrote her six years ago after she helped me move.

The format also forces a useful discipline. Because a card is short, you have to choose the one specific thing worth mentioning. Not "thanks for everything," but "the soup you left on the porch the week I was sick made that whole week bearable." Specifics are what make gratitude feel real.

I keep a small stack of plain cards and a pen in my desk drawer, so there is never an excuse of having nothing to write on. Three or four sentences is plenty. I try to write within a day or two, while the feeling is fresh.

It costs a few minutes and a stamp. In exchange, someone learns their effort mattered. That seems like a bargain.
