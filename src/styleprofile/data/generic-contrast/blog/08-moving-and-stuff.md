# What Moving Across the Country Taught Me About Stuff

Two years ago my partner and I moved from a small city in the Northeast to a mid-sized one on the West Coast, about twenty-seven hundred miles away. We had lived in our old apartment for nine years. When we started packing, I believed we were reasonably tidy people who did not own much. By the end of the second week, standing in a hallway surrounded by boxes, I understood how wrong I had been.

The first shock was the sheer volume. Nine years is long enough for a household to accumulate things without anyone noticing. Drawers I had not opened in years held cables for devices we no longer owned. A closet contained three umbrellas, two broken lamps, and a bag of fabric scraps intended for a quilting project that never happened. In the kitchen I counted eleven mugs, though we are two people who mostly drink from the same two. Each item had once seemed reasonable. Together, they were a kind of sediment.

Moving long-distance changes the economics of ownership in a way that local moves do not. When you move across town, keeping something costs you almost nothing; you throw it in a car and sort it out later. When you move across a continent, every object has a price. We got quotes from movers, and the estimate was calculated by weight and volume. Suddenly I could see exactly what a bookshelf cost to keep, and what that stack of old textbooks added to the total. The question shifted from "might I use this someday?" to "would I pay forty dollars to carry this across the country?" That second question turned out to be remarkably clarifying.

We decided to downsize aggressively. Over about six weeks we sold furniture, donated clothing, gave books to friends, and made many trips to the recycling center. The process was tiring, but it was also oddly emotional. Objects carry stories, and letting go of them felt a little like letting go of the moments they represented. I held a chipped ceramic bowl for several minutes, remembering the friend who gave it to us, before putting it in the donate pile. I realized the bowl was not the friendship. The friendship would survive without it.

That realization was probably the most important one. I had been treating belongings as a kind of external memory, as though discarding the souvenir might erase the experience. But memories, it turns out, are not stored in objects. A photograph of the bowl, or just the thought of the friend, did the job. I kept a small box of things that truly mattered to me: letters, a few photographs, my grandfather's pocket watch. Everything else was a candidate for release.

The logistics taught me something too. Sorting by room did not work, because every room contained the same mixture of essential, sentimental, and pointless. What worked was sorting by decision. I made three piles for each area: keep, release, and undecided. The undecided pile was always the largest at first, and I found that walking away for a day shrank it by half. Fatigue, I noticed, makes everything seem precious. Rested, I was far more willing to part with things. We also set a firm rule that nobody could rescue something from the other person's release pile without a spoken reason, which saved us from a surprising number of small arguments and made both of us more honest about what we truly wanted.

I also noticed how much of our stuff had been bought out of aspiration rather than need. There was an expensive pasta machine used twice, a pile of hobby supplies for a version of myself who painted on weekends, and a set of dumbbells that had mostly served as a doorstop. These objects were not really possessions; they were small guilt generators, reminding me of plans I had not carried out. Giving them away felt strangely like forgiveness. I was admitting that I was not going to become that person, and that this was fine.

When the truck finally left, we had roughly half the belongings we started with. The new apartment arrived with its own surprises. It was smaller than the old one, and I worried we had miscalculated. But with fewer things, the rooms felt larger. Mornings were simpler because I could find what I needed. Cleaning took half the time. I had not expected the lightness to be physical, but it was; there was a sort of ease in moving through a space that was not crowded with obligations.

Of course, stuff has a way of creeping back. In the past two years we have acquired new furniture, more books, and a regrettable number of kitchen gadgets. I would be lying if I claimed to have reached some permanent minimalist peace. But I do things differently now. Before buying something, I ask whether I would be willing to pack it into a box and carry it a long way. If the answer is no, I usually leave it on the shelf. I also try to practice a gentle one-in, one-out rule, which works about seventy percent of the time.

I have also grown more skeptical of the idea that comfort comes from accumulation. Some of the happiest moments in our first months in the new city happened in a nearly empty apartment, sitting on the floor eating takeout while we waited for furniture to arrive. We had a mattress, a few boxes, and each other, and it was plenty.

I do not want to romanticize this. Moving was expensive, exhausting, and at times lonely, and nothing about shedding possessions makes it painless. Some things I gave away I later missed, and a few I replaced at greater cost than keeping them would have been. Perfect decisions are impossible in the middle of packing.

Still, I would recommend the exercise to anyone, even if you never leave your zip code. Imagine you had to move everything very far, and see what you would actually take. The answer says a lot about what you value, and the rest is just weight.
