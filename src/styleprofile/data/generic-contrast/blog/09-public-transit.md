# The Quiet Appeal of Public Transit

Every weekday morning I walk six minutes to a bus stop, tap a card against a reader, and find a seat by the window. For the next thirty-five minutes, I do not steer, brake, or worry about parking. I read, or I stare out the glass, or I let my thoughts drift wherever they want. I used to drive to work and considered that normal. Now I think of those bus rides as one of the better parts of my day, and I have been trying to work out why.

The most obvious reason is the money. Owning a car is expensive in ways that are easy to ignore, because the costs arrive in separate pieces: insurance, fuel, registration, repairs, parking, and the slow loss of value. When I finally added everything up for the year before I sold mine, the figure was uncomfortable. A monthly transit pass costs a small fraction of that, and the difference quietly paid for a vacation. I do not want to make this sound like pure sacrifice. Spending less and traveling more was an excellent trade.

But money is not what keeps me on the bus. What I value more is the way transit hands back a block of time. Driving demands your full attention, so those minutes are spent, in a sense, on managing risk. On a bus or train, the same minutes are available to me. In the past year I have finished roughly forty books during my commute. I have also answered messages, planned meals, and made lists without feeling that I was stealing time from anything else. On days when I have nothing to do, I simply look out the window, and that has its own value.

There is also something I can only describe as a gentle civic pleasure in riding with other people. A bus is one of the few places where a cross-section of a city shares an enclosed space for a while. Students with headphones sit near retirees carrying grocery bags. Construction workers in dusty boots ride alongside people in business clothes. Nobody is particularly friendly, and nobody is hostile either. We simply coexist, each headed somewhere, and I find that oddly reassuring. It reminds me that a city is made of many ordinary lives running in parallel.

Small, quiet moments happen too. Last winter an elderly man boarded with a heavy bag, and three different passengers stood to offer him a seat. A driver I see regularly now greets me by name and sometimes waits a few seconds when she sees me jogging toward the stop. These things are minor, but they accumulate into a sense of belonging that I never felt behind the wheel, sealed inside my own little compartment.

I should be fair about the downsides, because transit is not magic. Buses run late. Service on weekends and evenings is thinner, which makes spontaneous plans harder. In bad weather the wait for a connection can be miserable, and there have been mornings when I stood at the stop in sleet, wondering whether I had made a terrible mistake. If you live somewhere with sparse routes, relying on public transit may be difficult or impossible, and I do not think people in that situation are failing at anything. The system has to be good enough to use before anyone can be asked to use it.

That is really the larger point. Whether transit feels pleasant depends enormously on how well it is built and funded. Frequent service, reliable schedules, clean stations, and safe stops make an enormous difference. Where those exist, people ride in large numbers, and where they do not, people understandably drive. I live along a route that runs every ten minutes, and I suspect that frequency is the single feature that makes my whole experience possible. If the bus came once an hour, I would still have a car.

There are broader benefits that I appreciate even though I can only partly measure them. Fewer cars on the road means less congestion, less noise, and cleaner air. A street with a good bus line can support shops and cafes within walking distance, which makes a neighborhood livelier. I notice these effects in my own area, where small businesses cluster around the stops, and where I run into neighbors without planning to. A place built around transit tends to feel more connected, even if each individual trip is unremarkable.

Personally, I have also become a better walker. Transit trips begin and end on foot, so my daily routine now includes close to three miles of walking that I never scheduled. I sleep better, I feel more energetic, and I know my neighborhood far more intimately than I did when I passed through it at thirty miles an hour. I have discovered a bakery, a tiny park, and a shortcut through an alley, all because I was walking instead of driving.

None of this means I will never own a car again. I may well buy one someday, if my circumstances change. Plenty of people need vehicles for work, family, or disability, and nothing in my enthusiasm is meant to dismiss that. I rent one when I need it, which happens a few times a year, and the arrangement suits me.

For now, though, I am content to be a regular on the 14 bus. If you have been curious, I would suggest trying it for a month, with a real commitment and not just a single experimental ride. Give yourself time to learn the schedule, bring something to read, and see how you feel. You may find, as I did, that the commute stops being the part of the day you endure and becomes a small stretch of calm that belongs to you.
