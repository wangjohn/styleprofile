# A Letter at the Close of 2026

December 2026

Dear friends and supporters of Open Door Literacy,

Last Tuesday evening I stayed late at our Fenwick Street center to lock up, and found a man in his fifties still sitting at a table in the back room, working through a children's picture book with a pencil. He had come in for his tutoring session two hours earlier. I asked if everything was all right. He looked up and said, "I'm reading this one to my granddaughter on Saturday. I want to get it right." Then he went back to the page.

I have thought about that moment more than almost anything else this year. It captures what we do, and why it matters, better than any statistic I could give you. But I will give you some statistics anyway, because you deserve to know what your generosity accomplished in 2026.

## The year in numbers

- **412 adult learners** worked with us this year, up from 331 in 2025.
- **288 volunteer tutors** gave more than 14,000 hours. If we paid for that time at even a modest hourly rate, the figure would be larger than our entire annual budget.
- **67 learners** earned their high school equivalency credential, nineteen more than last year.
- **41 families** joined our new Read Together program, in which parents and caregivers learn alongside their young children.
- **Two new sites** opened, in the Marlowe Heights library and the Riverside Community Hall, bringing us closer to neighborhoods where transportation has been a real barrier.

Behind each of these figures is a person with a story, a schedule full of obligations, and a good deal of courage. Many of our learners work two jobs. Several are raising children on their own. Some spent decades hiding the fact that they could not read well, and walking through our door was the hardest thing they have ever done.

## What went well

I want to start with what worked, because we do not celebrate enough.

Our biggest success was the launch of Read Together in March. We had piloted a version of it in 2025 with just eight families and were not certain it would grow. By June we had a waiting list. The model is simple: parents and children attend a shared hour on Saturday mornings, with a literacy coach guiding the adults while a children's reading specialist works with their kids. Then everyone reunites for a story. The results have surprised us. Parents report reading to their children more often, and several have told us their kids now correct their pronunciation with great seriousness.

We also finally replaced our aging intake system. For years, our records lived in a patchwork of spreadsheets and paper binders, which meant staff spent hours chasing information that should have taken minutes. Thanks to a technology grant from the Whitcomb Foundation and the patient work of our program manager, Delphine Okonkwo, every learner now has a single, secure profile. Tutors can see progress notes before a session instead of asking the same questions twice. It sounds mundane, but it has given our staff back an estimated eight hours each week.

And our tutor community deepened. We started monthly gatherings where volunteers share what is working, swap lesson ideas, and simply talk. Retention among new tutors rose from 61 percent to 79 percent. People stay when they feel connected, and we have learned that connection must be built on purpose.

## What was hard

It would be dishonest to pretend the year was easy.

In April, a funding agreement we had counted on for three years ended without renewal, leaving a gap of roughly $85,000. We spent several tense weeks deciding what to cut. In the end we paused our evening workplace classes at two sites, and I know some learners were disappointed. We are working to bring them back in the spring, but I will not promise a date until the money is secure.

We also lost a dear colleague. Harold Brandt, who tutored with us for eleven years and served on our board for six, passed away in September. Harold believed that nobody was too old to learn and that no one should ever be made to feel embarrassed. He once spent four months helping a 72-year-old woman write a single letter to her sister. When she read it aloud to the whole class, there was not a dry eye in the room. We have named our tutor training room in his honor.

Finally, demand continues to outpace our capacity. As I write, 138 people are on our waiting list. Each of them is waiting for an opening, which means waiting for a tutor, a room, and a few hours that someone could give. This number weighs on me.

## What we are learning

Three lessons stood out this year.

First, adult learners do best when learning is tied directly to what they care about. The man with the picture book is a good example. Our strongest results come when tutors start from a learner's goal, whether that is reading a lease, passing a driving test, helping with homework, or filling out a job application, and build the skills around it.

Second, small barriers matter enormously. Bus schedules, childcare, and work shifts all influence whether someone returns the following week. This is why we added a small transportation fund and why Read Together offers a children's program alongside the adult one. Removing a single obstacle can keep a person in the program.

Third, we cannot do this alone. Our new partnerships with the public library system, the Eastside Workforce Center, and three local churches have extended our reach in ways that our small staff never could have managed. I am grateful to every partner who has said yes.

## Looking ahead to 2027

Here is what we hope to accomplish next year:

1. Restore the evening workplace classes and add a third site on the north side.
2. Serve at least 500 learners and cut our waiting list by half.
3. Launch a digital literacy track, because so many everyday tasks, from banking to scheduling a doctor's appointment, now take place on a screen.
4. Raise $120,000 to build a reserve fund, so a single lost grant never forces us to turn people away again.

These goals are ambitious, and I do not say that lightly. They depend on the same things that carried us this year: staff who give far more than their job descriptions require, tutors who show up in the rain, and donors who believe that reading is not a luxury but a doorway.

## A final word

On Saturday, that man did read his book to his granddaughter. He told me about it on Monday, grinning, and said she asked him to read it a second time. He did not stumble once.

That is what you made possible. Thank you for walking with us this year, and for the faith you have placed in the people we serve. I wish you and your family a peaceful holiday and a bright new year.

With sincere gratitude,

Marianne Lindqvist
Executive Director, Open Door Literacy
