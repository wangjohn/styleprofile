# How a Mortgage Amortizes

When you take out a fixed-rate mortgage, your monthly payment stays the same for decades, yet the loan balance does not fall evenly. Early payments go mostly toward interest, and later payments go mostly toward principal. That shifting split is what amortization describes.

## What "amortize" means

To amortize a loan is to pay it off gradually through scheduled payments. Each payment covers two things:

- **Interest:** the cost of borrowing, calculated on the balance you still owe
- **Principal:** the portion that actually reduces the balance

The payment amount is set so that after the final payment, the balance reaches exactly zero.

## How each payment is split

Every month, the lender takes these steps:

1. Multiply the current balance by the monthly interest rate (the annual rate divided by 12).
2. Subtract that interest from your fixed payment.
3. Apply whatever is left to principal.
4. Lower the balance by that amount, and repeat next month.

Because interest is charged on the remaining balance, a big balance generates a big interest charge. As the balance shrinks, the interest portion shrinks, and more of the same payment flows to principal.

## A worked example

Suppose you borrow $300,000 for 30 years at a 6% fixed rate.

- Monthly rate: 0.5%
- Monthly payment (principal and interest only): about $1,799

**Month 1**
- Interest: $300,000 × 0.005 = $1,500
- Principal: $1,799 − $1,500 = $299
- New balance: about $299,701

**Month 2**
- Interest: about $1,498.50
- Principal: about $300.50

The principal share grows only slightly each month at first. By roughly year 20, the split looks very different, with principal making up a much larger part of each payment. In the final year, nearly all of the payment reduces principal.

## The payment formula

Lenders calculate the fixed payment with this formula:

M = P × [r(1 + r)^n] / [(1 + r)^n − 1]

- **M** is the monthly payment
- **P** is the loan amount
- **r** is the monthly interest rate
- **n** is the total number of payments (360 for a 30-year loan)

You rarely need to compute it by hand, since online calculators and spreadsheets (the PMT function) do this instantly.

## Total interest over the life of the loan

In the example above, 360 payments of about $1,799 add up to roughly $647,000. Subtract the $300,000 borrowed, and you will have paid about $347,000 in interest, more than the original loan. That surprises many borrowers, and it shows how much time matters.

## How loan length changes the picture

| Term | Monthly payment | Total interest (approx.) |
|------|----------------|--------------------------|
| 30 years at 6% | $1,799 | $347,000 |
| 15 years at 5.5% | $2,451 | $141,000 |

A shorter term raises the monthly payment but cuts total interest dramatically, partly because the rate is often lower and partly because you borrow for fewer years.

## Building equity

**Equity** is the portion of your home you own outright: its value minus what you owe. In the early years, equity grows slowly because little principal is being paid down. Rising home prices can add equity faster than your payments do, and falling prices can erase it.

## Ways to change the schedule

- **Extra principal payments:** Any additional amount applied to principal reduces the balance right away, so future interest is calculated on a smaller number. Even $100 a month can shorten a 30-year loan by several years.
- **Biweekly payments:** Paying half your payment every two weeks results in 26 half-payments, or 13 full payments a year, which speeds payoff.
- **Refinancing:** Replacing your loan with a new one at a lower rate can reduce interest, but it restarts the amortization clock unless you choose a shorter term. Closing costs also matter.
- **Recasting:** Some lenders let you make a large lump-sum payment and then recalculate your monthly payment based on the lower balance.

When making extra payments, tell your lender they should go toward principal, and check that there are no prepayment penalties.

## What is not part of amortization

Your actual monthly housing bill usually includes more than principal and interest. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, and sometimes private mortgage insurance are often collected in an escrow account. These costs do not amortize and can change from year to year, so your total payment may rise even though your principal and interest stays fixed.

## Adjustable-rate mortgages

With an adjustable-rate mortgage, the interest rate resets after an initial period. The lender recalculates the payment based on the remaining balance, the new rate, and the remaining term, so the payment can go up or down.

## Reading an amortization schedule

An amortization schedule is a table listing each payment with its interest, principal, and remaining balance. Most lenders provide one at closing. Looking at yours helps you see how much equity you have built, what extra payments would save, and when you would reach milestones such as 20 percent equity.
