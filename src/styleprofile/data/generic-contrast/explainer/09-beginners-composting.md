# A Beginner's Guide to Composting

Composting turns kitchen scraps and yard waste into dark, crumbly, nutrient-rich material that improves soil. It reduces what goes to the landfill, cuts down on the need for store-bought fertilizer, and is far easier than it sounds. This guide covers the basics so you can start with confidence.

## What compost actually is

Compost is organic matter that has been broken down by microorganisms, along with worms, insects, and fungi. Given the right conditions, these decomposers consume plant and food material and leave behind **humus**, a stable, earthy substance that feeds plants and helps soil hold water.

Nothing mysterious needs to be added. The microbes are already present in soil, leaves, and food scraps. Your job is to give them a comfortable environment.

## Why bother?

- **Less waste:** Food scraps and yard trimmings make up a large share of household garbage.
- **Better soil:** Compost loosens clay, helps sandy soil retain moisture, and adds nutrients slowly.
- **Fewer emissions:** Organic matter rotting in landfills without oxygen produces methane, a potent greenhouse gas. Composting with air produces far less.
- **Savings:** You make a free soil amendment instead of buying bagged products.

## The four ingredients

Every compost pile needs balance among four things:

1. **Browns (carbon):** dry, woody, or fibrous materials
2. **Greens (nitrogen):** fresh, moist, or protein-rich materials
3. **Water:** enough to keep things damp
4. **Air:** oxygen for the microbes that do the work

### Browns

- Dry leaves
- Shredded cardboard and paper
- Straw or hay
- Sawdust from untreated wood
- Wood chips and small twigs
- Paper towels and napkins (unbleached, no chemical cleaners)

### Greens

- Fruit and vegetable scraps
- Coffee grounds and paper filters
- Tea bags (check that they do not contain plastic)
- Fresh grass clippings
- Plant trimmings and weeds without seeds
- Eggshells (technically a mineral source, but fine to add)

### The ratio

A common guideline is about **two to three parts browns to one part greens by volume**. You do not need to measure precisely. If the pile smells bad or looks soggy, add more browns. If it is dry and not breaking down, add more greens or water.

## What to leave out

Some materials cause odors, attract pests, or carry pathogens:

- Meat, fish, and bones
- Dairy products and greasy foods
- Pet waste from cats and dogs
- Diseased plants
- Weeds that have gone to seed or invasive runners
- Coal or charcoal ash
- Treated or painted wood
- Glossy or coated paper

Some municipal programs and bokashi systems do accept meat and dairy, but for a basic backyard pile, avoid them.

## Choosing a method

### Outdoor pile or bin

The most traditional approach. A simple pile works, but a bin keeps things neat and holds in heat.

- **Size:** About 3 feet by 3 feet by 3 feet is ideal for generating heat.
- **Location:** A level, well-drained spot with partial shade. Direct contact with soil lets worms and microbes move in.
- **Options:** Wooden pallets, wire mesh, or a store-bought bin.

### Tumbler

A sealed drum on a stand that you rotate. Tumblers are tidy, pest-resistant, and easy to turn, but they hold less and can run wet.

### Worm bin (vermicomposting)

Red wiggler worms eat food scraps in a container, working well indoors or on a balcony. It handles small amounts of kitchen waste and produces rich worm castings.

### Countertop or bokashi system

Bokashi uses an inoculated bran to ferment food waste in a sealed bucket. The result is pre-compost that is buried or added to a pile. It can handle foods that traditional composting avoids.

### Municipal or community programs

If you lack space, many cities offer curbside pickup or drop-off sites for food scraps.

## Step-by-step: starting a backyard pile

1. **Pick the spot and set up the bin.**
2. **Lay down a base** of coarse twigs or straw a few inches thick for airflow.
3. **Add layers or mix:** alternate browns and greens, starting and ending with browns.
4. **Chop large items.** Smaller pieces break down faster.
5. **Moisten.** The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge.
6. **Cover** with a lid or tarp to hold moisture and keep out heavy rain.
7. **Turn it** every one to two weeks with a pitchfork to add oxygen.

## Keeping the pile healthy

- **Temperature:** An active pile heats up, sometimes reaching 130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit in the center. Heat speeds decomposition and kills many weed seeds. It will cool as the food supply runs low.
- **Moisture:** Squeeze a handful. A few drops of water should appear, no more.
- **Aeration:** Turning or poking holes keeps oxygen flowing. A compacted pile goes anaerobic and smells.
- **Particle size:** Shredding leaves with a mower or chopping scraps boosts surface area for microbes.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| Bad, rotten smell | Too wet or too many greens | Add browns, turn the pile |
| Ammonia smell | Too much nitrogen | Add browns, mix well |
| Pile is dry and nothing happens | Not enough moisture | Add water and greens |
| Pile is damp but cold | Too small or lacking nitrogen | Add greens, enlarge the pile |
| Flies | Exposed food scraps | Bury scraps in the center, cover with browns |
| Rodents | Meat or oily food, open bin | Remove those items, use a closed bin with mesh |

## How long does it take?

Timing depends on effort and conditions:

- **Active, hot composting** with regular turning: roughly 1 to 3 months
- **Passive composting** with little turning: 6 months to 2 years
- **Worm bins:** about 2 to 4 months

Compost is ready when it is dark brown, crumbly, smells like fresh earth, and original materials are mostly unrecognizable. A few twigs or eggshell fragments are fine.

## Using finished compost

- **Garden beds:** Spread one to two inches on top and work it lightly into the soil.
- **Potting mix:** Blend about one part compost with two to three parts soil or other medium.
- **Lawns:** Sprinkle a thin layer and rake it in.
- **Mulch:** Spread around trees and shrubs, keeping it away from trunks.
- **Compost tea:** Steep in water for a mild liquid feed.

If compost is not fully finished, it can compete with plants for nitrogen, so allow it to cure for a few weeks before using it on seedlings.

## Common beginner mistakes

- Adding too many greens at once, which leads to a smelly, slimy pile
- Ignoring moisture, either letting it dry out or soak
- Burying a pile that never gets turned or aerated
- Adding meat or dairy and attracting animals
- Expecting instant results

## A simple routine to follow

- Keep a lidded container in the kitchen for scraps.
- Empty it into the bin every few days, adding a layer of browns each time.
- Turn the pile every week or two.
- Check moisture and adjust as needed.
- Harvest finished compost from the bottom when it is ready.

Composting rewards patience more than precision. Start small, pay attention to how the pile looks and smells, and adjust as you learn. Within a season, you will likely have a dependable supply of homemade soil booster.
