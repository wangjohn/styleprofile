# How Tides Work

Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level along coastlines. They come mainly from the gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser degree, the Sun, combined with the rotation of the Earth.

## The Moon's pull

Gravity weakens with distance. The side of Earth facing the Moon is closer to it and feels a slightly stronger pull than the planet's center, while the far side feels a slightly weaker pull. The ocean, being fluid, responds to this difference by bulging.

- A **bulge toward the Moon** forms on the near side, where water is pulled more strongly than the Earth beneath it.
- A **second bulge on the opposite side** forms because the solid Earth is pulled toward the Moon more than the far water is, leaving that water "behind."

These bulges are the high tides. The areas between them experience low tides.

## Why most coasts have two tides a day

Earth rotates once every 24 hours, so a given point on the coast passes through both bulges and both low areas. Meanwhile, the Moon orbits Earth in the same direction, which means it takes about 24 hours and 50 minutes for the Moon to return to the same position in the sky. As a result:

- High tides occur roughly every 12 hours and 25 minutes.
- Tide times shift about 50 minutes later each day.

## The Sun's role

The Sun is far larger than the Moon, but it is also about 390 times farther away. Its tidal influence is roughly 46 percent as strong as the Moon's. The two effects add together or partly cancel depending on how they line up.

### Spring tides

When the Sun, Moon, and Earth are aligned, at **new moon** and **full moon**, their pulls reinforce one another. This produces **spring tides**, with higher highs and lower lows. The name has nothing to do with the season; it comes from the idea of the water "springing up."

### Neap tides

At the **first and third quarter moons**, the Sun and Moon pull at right angles. Their effects partly offset, creating **neap tides**, which have a smaller range between high and low water.

This cycle repeats about every two weeks.

## Types of daily tide patterns

Not every coast follows the same rhythm:

- **Semidiurnal:** two high and two low tides of similar height each day, common on the US Atlantic coast.
- **Diurnal:** one high and one low per day, found in places like parts of the Gulf of Mexico.
- **Mixed semidiurnal:** two highs and two lows of noticeably different heights, typical on the US Pacific coast.

## Why tidal range varies so much

The size of the tide depends on far more than astronomy. Local factors include:

- **Shape of the coastline.** Funnel-shaped bays squeeze incoming water into a narrow space and amplify the range. The Bay of Fundy in Canada can see differences of more than 15 meters (about 50 feet).
- **Depth of the seafloor.** Shallow areas slow and reshape tidal waves.
- **Size of the ocean basin.** Water in a basin sloshes back and forth at a natural rhythm, which can match or clash with tidal forcing.
- **Earth's rotation.** The Coriolis effect bends currents, creating rotating patterns around points in the ocean where there is almost no tide.

Small enclosed seas like the Mediterranean and Baltic have very small tides because they are too small for the Moon's pull to produce much bulging.

## Orbital variations

The Moon's orbit is not a perfect circle.

- At **perigee**, when the Moon is closest to Earth, tides are stronger.
- At **apogee**, when it is farthest, they are weaker.
- When a perigee coincides with a new or full moon, the result is sometimes called a **perigean spring tide** or "king tide," and it can raise the risk of coastal flooding, especially when combined with storms.

The Earth is also closest to the Sun in early January, which slightly strengthens the solar contribution around that time.

## Tidal currents

As the tide rises and falls, water must move horizontally to fill or drain areas. These tidal currents are strongest in narrow channels and inlets. A **flood tide** is the incoming, rising tide, and an **ebb tide** is the outgoing, falling one. Moments of calm between them are called **slack water**.

## Common misconceptions

- **The Moon does not pull water "up" like a vacuum.** It is the difference in pull across the planet that creates the bulges.
- **Tides are not waves** in the sense of surf. They are extremely long waves, with wavelengths spanning thousands of kilometers.
- **Lakes have tides too**, but they are tiny, usually just a few centimeters, because lakes are small.
- **The ocean does not sit still while the Earth turns.** Friction, coastlines, and basin shapes make the real picture more complicated than two simple bulges.

## Why tides matter

- **Navigation:** Ships and boats use tide tables to enter harbors safely.
- **Ecology:** The intertidal zone supports species that live with cycles of exposure and submersion.
- **Energy:** Tidal power plants capture energy from moving water.
- **Fishing and recreation:** Beachcombing, surfing, and clamming all depend on timing.

If you spend time near the coast, a tide chart is a good habit to build. Knowing when water will rise or fall helps you plan, stay safe, and notice the Moon's quiet influence on the shoreline.
