# How Vaccines Train the Immune System

A vaccine gives your body a safe preview of a germ so that it can build defenses before the real thing shows up. The process borrows from how your immune system already responds to infection, minus the illness.

## The immune system in brief

Your body has two broad layers of defense:

- **Innate immunity:** fast, general-purpose responses such as inflammation and white blood cells that engulf invaders. It reacts the same way every time.
- **Adaptive immunity:** slower to start but highly specific. It learns to recognize particular pathogens and remembers them.

Vaccines work on the adaptive layer.

## Antigens: the training target

Every virus or bacterium carries molecules called **antigens**, which act like identifying badges. A vaccine delivers an antigen, or instructions for making one, without the ability to cause serious disease.

## What happens after the shot

1. **Detection.** Immune cells near the injection site pick up the antigen and carry it to nearby lymph nodes.
2. **Activation.** Helper T cells signal other cells that something foreign is present.
3. **Antibody production.** B cells make antibodies, proteins shaped to latch onto that specific antigen.
4. **Killer T cells.** Some T cells learn to destroy cells that have been infected.
5. **Memory.** After the response settles, memory B and T cells remain, sometimes for years or decades.

If you later meet the real pathogen, those memory cells respond within hours or days instead of weeks, often stopping the infection before symptoms begin.

## Types of vaccines

- **Live-attenuated:** a weakened form of the germ (for example, measles, mumps, and rubella).
- **Inactivated:** a killed germ that cannot replicate (for example, some polio and flu vaccines).
- **Subunit and conjugate:** only a piece of the germ, such as a protein or sugar coating.
- **Toxoid:** a defanged version of a toxin, used for tetanus and diphtheria.
- **mRNA:** instructions that let your cells briefly produce an antigen, which the immune system then learns to recognize. The mRNA breaks down quickly and does not alter your DNA.
- **Viral vector:** a harmless virus that carries genetic instructions for an antigen.

## Why boosters and multiple doses exist

Some vaccines need more than one dose to build strong memory. Others need boosters because protection fades over time or because pathogens, like influenza, change from year to year.

## Why you might feel unwell afterward

A sore arm, mild fever, or fatigue usually means the immune system is responding. These effects typically pass within a day or two.

## Protecting others

When enough people are immune, the germ has trouble spreading. This is called **herd immunity**, and it helps shield infants and people who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons.
