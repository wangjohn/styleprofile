# How to Prepare for Your First Marathon

Running 26.2 miles is a big goal, but it is very achievable for most healthy adults who follow a patient, structured plan. Preparation is mostly about building endurance gradually, practicing race-day habits, and avoiding injury.

## Start with the right foundation

Before beginning a training plan, aim to have:

- A base of running regularly for at least three to six months
- Comfort with running about 10 to 15 miles per week
- A recent check-up if you have health conditions or have been inactive

Most beginner plans last 16 to 20 weeks. Pick a race with enough time to complete the full plan.

## Build a weekly structure

A typical beginner week has four or five training days:

- **Easy runs (2 to 3 days):** conversational pace, shorter distances
- **Long run (1 day):** the key workout, increasing gradually each week
- **Rest or cross-training (2 to 3 days):** cycling, swimming, or strength work

Most of your running should feel easy. If you cannot speak in short sentences, you are probably going too fast.

## The long run

The long run teaches your body to handle time on your feet. Increase it by about one to two miles per week, and include a "cutback" week every third or fourth week where mileage drops by roughly 20 to 30 percent. Most plans peak at 18 to 22 miles about three weeks before race day.

Do not worry about running the full 26.2 miles in training. The crowd, adrenaline, and taper will carry you the rest of the way.

## Follow the 10 percent guideline carefully

Raising weekly mileage by about 10 percent at a time is a common rule of thumb to limit overuse injuries. It is a guide, not a law, but jumping too quickly is the most common beginner mistake.

## Fuel and hydrate

- **Daily eating:** Prioritize carbohydrates for energy, adequate protein for recovery, and enough overall calories.
- **During long runs:** Practice taking in 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour with gels, chews, or sports drink, starting after the first 45 to 60 minutes.
- **Hydration:** Drink to thirst, and consider electrolytes on hot days.
- **Test everything in training.** Never try a new food or drink on race day.

## Gear and shoes

- Visit a running store for shoes that fit well, with about a thumb's width of room in the toe box.
- Replace shoes every 300 to 500 miles.
- Wear moisture-wicking socks and clothing, and use anti-chafe balm where needed.
- Wear your planned race outfit on at least one long run.

## Listen to your body

Sore muscles are normal. Sharp or worsening pain, swelling, or pain that changes your stride is not. If something hurts for more than a few days, take rest days, see a physical therapist or doctor, and adjust your plan. Missing a few runs will not ruin your training, but running through an injury can.

## Sleep and recovery

Training stress becomes fitness only during recovery. Aim for seven to nine hours of sleep, add easy mobility work or stretching, and take your rest days seriously.

## The taper

In the last two to three weeks, reduce mileage while keeping some short, easy efforts. You may feel restless or sluggish, which is normal. The taper lets your muscles repair and glycogen stores fill up.

## Race-week checklist

- Lay out clothes, bib, shoes, and fuel the night before
- Eat familiar carbohydrate-rich meals in the final two days
- Arrive early and plan transportation
- Review the course map and aid station locations

## Pacing on race day

Start slower than you think you should. Going out too fast is the classic mistake, and it often costs far more time in the final miles than it saves early. Aim for even or slightly negative splits, where the second half is as fast as or a bit faster than the first.

- Walk through aid stations if needed to drink safely
- Take fuel on schedule, even if you feel good
- Break the race into sections, such as 10 miles, 10 miles, then the last 10K

## Mental preparation

The final six miles are often the hardest. Prepare by practicing positive self-talk, breaking long runs into segments, and deciding in advance what you will do when the going gets rough. Many runners find a mantra or a favorite playlist helpful.

## After the finish

Keep moving gently, eat and drink, and put on warm clothes. Expect soreness for several days. Wait at least a week or two before returning to hard running, and consider a recovery period of easy activity.

Finishing a first marathon is less about speed than about consistency. Show up for the workouts, respect the rest, and let the miles accumulate.
