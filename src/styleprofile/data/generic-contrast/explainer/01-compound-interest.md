# How Compound Interest Works

Compound interest means you earn interest not only on the money you originally put in, but also on the interest that money has already earned. Over long periods, this turns steady saving into surprisingly large totals.

## The basic idea

- **Simple interest** is paid only on the original amount (the principal).
- **Compound interest** is paid on the principal plus all interest accumulated so far.

Each time interest is added, the base that earns the next round of interest gets bigger.

## The formula

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)

- **P** is the starting principal
- **r** is the annual interest rate, as a decimal
- **n** is how many times per year interest compounds
- **t** is the number of years
- **A** is the final amount

## A quick example

Put $1,000 in an account earning 5% a year, compounded annually:

- Year 1: $1,050.00
- Year 2: $1,102.50
- Year 10: about $1,629
- Year 30: about $4,322

Simple interest would give only $2,500 after 30 years.

## What changes the outcome

- **Time:** Starting earlier matters most.
- **Rate:** Higher rates speed growth.
- **Frequency:** Monthly beats annual, slightly.
- **Contributions:** Each deposit also compounds.

## The flip side

Compounding works against you with debt, such as credit card balances.

A handy shortcut is the Rule of 72: divide 72 by your annual rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 6%, that is about 12 years.
