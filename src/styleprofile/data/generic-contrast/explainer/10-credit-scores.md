# How Credit Scores Are Calculated

A credit score is a three-digit number that summarizes how likely you are to repay borrowed money. Lenders, landlords, insurers, and sometimes employers use it to decide whether to approve you and what terms to offer. Understanding what goes into it makes it much easier to improve.

## Where scores come from

Your score is generated from the information in your **credit reports**, which are compiled by the three major bureaus: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Lenders and creditors report your account activity to these bureaus, and scoring companies run that data through mathematical models.

The two best-known scoring companies are:

- **FICO:** used in most lending decisions, with many versions
- **VantageScore:** created jointly by the three bureaus, and common in free consumer apps

You actually have many scores, not one. Different models, bureaus, and industry-specific versions (auto, credit card, mortgage) produce slightly different numbers. They tend to move in the same direction, though.

## The score range

Most models use a range of **300 to 850**. Rough categories for FICO scores:

- **Below 580:** poor
- **580 to 669:** fair
- **670 to 739:** good
- **740 to 799:** very good
- **800 and above:** exceptional

Higher scores generally mean lower interest rates and better approval odds.

## The five factors in a FICO score

FICO publishes the approximate weight of each category.

| Factor | Approximate weight |
|--------|-------------------|
| Payment history | 35% |
| Amounts owed | 30% |
| Length of credit history | 15% |
| New credit | 10% |
| Credit mix | 10% |

### 1. Payment history (35%)

The single most important factor. It asks whether you pay on time. The model looks at:

- Late or missed payments, and how late they were (30, 60, 90 days or more)
- Accounts sent to collections
- Bankruptcies, foreclosures, and judgments
- How recent the problems were and how many occurred

A single 30-day late payment can drop a good score noticeably, and the impact fades gradually over time. Negative items typically remain on reports for about seven years, while bankruptcies can stay up to ten.

### 2. Amounts owed (30%)

This category looks at how much debt you carry, especially relative to your limits.

- **Credit utilization** is your revolving balances divided by your total revolving credit limits. If you have $2,000 in balances on $10,000 of limits, your utilization is 20 percent.
- Lower is generally better. Many experts suggest keeping it under 30 percent, and people with the highest scores often stay under 10 percent.
- Utilization is measured both overall and per card, so one maxed-out card can hurt even if your overall figure is low.
- Installment loan balances matter too, such as how much remains on a mortgage or car loan compared with the original amount.

Utilization has no long memory. Because it reflects your most recent reported balances, paying down cards can raise your score quickly.

### 3. Length of credit history (15%)

Longer histories give models more evidence. Factors include:

- Age of your oldest account
- Age of your newest account
- Average age of all accounts
- How long since specific accounts were used

This is one reason closing an old card can sometimes hurt: over time the account stops contributing to your average age once it drops off your report.

### 4. New credit (10%)

Opening many accounts in a short time can suggest financial stress. This category considers:

- **Hard inquiries:** when you apply for credit and a lender checks your report. Each typically lowers a score by a few points at most and counts for about a year, though inquiries stay visible for two.
- **Recently opened accounts:** these lower your average account age.

Rate shopping for a mortgage, auto loan, or student loan is generally treated as a single inquiry if done within a short window, often 14 to 45 days depending on the model. **Soft inquiries**, such as checking your own score or pre-approval offers, do not affect it.

### 5. Credit mix (10%)

Models like to see that you can handle different kinds of credit:

- **Revolving:** credit cards, lines of credit
- **Installment:** auto loans, mortgages, student loans, personal loans

You do not need to take on a loan you do not need just to improve this factor. It is the least influential category.

## What is not in your score

Credit scores do not include:

- Your income, salary, or employment history
- Your age, race, gender, religion, or marital status
- Your bank balances or savings
- Where you live
- Interest rates on your cards
- Whether you check your own score

Rent and utility payments historically did not appear on reports, though newer programs and some scoring models can include them if reported.

## How VantageScore differs

VantageScore uses similar information but weighs it differently and labels the categories by influence, such as payment history being highly influential and credit utilization moderately influential. It can also generate a score with a shorter history, as little as one month, while FICO traditionally requires at least six months of activity. Newer versions of both models treat paid collections and some medical debt more leniently.

## Why scores differ across sources

If you see different numbers on different sites, it is usually for one of these reasons:

- Different scoring models or versions
- Different bureaus, since not every creditor reports to all three
- Timing, because balances update at different moments
- Educational scores that are not used by lenders

A gap of 20 to 40 points is not unusual.

## Practical ways to build or improve a score

1. **Pay every bill on time.** Set up autopay for at least the minimum.
2. **Lower your utilization.** Pay down balances, ask for credit limit increases (without increasing spending), or make payments before the statement closes.
3. **Keep old accounts open** when they have no annual fee.
4. **Apply for credit sparingly.** Only open accounts you need.
5. **Check your reports.** You can get free reports from each bureau through AnnualCreditReport.com.
6. **Dispute errors.** Mistakes such as accounts that are not yours or incorrect late payments can be challenged with the bureau and the creditor.
7. **Start small if you have no history.** A secured credit card, credit-builder loan, or becoming an authorized user on a trusted person's account can establish a record.

## Myths worth dropping

- **"Carrying a balance helps my score."** It does not. Paying in full each month is best and saves interest.
- **"Checking my own credit hurts it."** Self-checks are soft inquiries.
- **"Closing cards raises my score."** Often the opposite occurs.
- **"Debit card use builds credit."** Debit cards do not report to the bureaus.

## How quickly can scores change?

Some changes show up within a billing cycle or two, such as a lower utilization rate after paying down a balance. Others take years, like the effect of a past delinquency fading or an account aging. Steady habits matter more than quick fixes.

## A short summary

Payment history and amounts owed together make up about two-thirds of the typical FICO score. If you pay on time, keep balances low relative to limits, avoid unnecessary new accounts, and let your history age, your score will tend to rise. The number is not a judgment of your worth, only a snapshot of your borrowing behavior, and it can be improved with time and consistency.
